Introduction to Human-Computer Interaction

Introduction

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the study and design of the points where people and technology meet. It asks how interfaces should be shaped so that real users, with real goals, limits, and contexts, can accomplish what they came to do. In this topic you will build the shared vocabulary of the field: what HCI is, the core principles that guide good interaction design, how the user-centered design process works, and where HCI activities fit inside the software development lifecycle.

This matters here because the course project will be judged not only on whether your software runs, but on whether it is usable, understandable, and appropriate for the people you design it for. Before you install tools or write a project plan, you need mental models for terms you will use all semester: usability, user experience, accessibility, iteration, prototyping, and evaluation. Getting these distinctions straight early prevents the common mistake of treating interface design as decoration added at the end.

As you watch, look for three things. First, notice how each speaker defines interaction design or UX and compare the definitions rather than memorizing one. Second, track the activities named in the design process (understanding users, establishing requirements, designing alternatives, prototyping, evaluating) and think about which ones your team can realistically run on the course project. Third, collect the examples of good and bad design that are mentioned, and ask yourself what principle each example illustrates.

Version Control with GitHub

Introduction

Version control is the practice of tracking changes to files over time so that you can see what changed, who changed it, and why — and roll back when something breaks. Git is the tool that does this tracking on your own machine; GitHub is the hosted platform that stores Git repositories online and adds collaboration features such as issues, pull requests, code review, and project boards. For the rest of this course, GitHub is where your team's prototype code, design assets, and documentation will live.

This topic matters because your course project is a team effort. Without a shared workflow, teammates overwrite each other's work, lose files, and lose track of who is responsible for what. A clean repository, a sensible branching strategy, and a habit of reviewing changes through pull requests will save your team far more time than it costs to set up in Module 1.

As you watch, keep three questions in mind. First, what is happening locally (on your computer) versus remotely (on GitHub)? Second, what is the minimum set of commands you actually need day to day — clone, add, commit, push, pull, branch, merge? Third, how does a pull request function as both a technical merge and a human conversation about the work? Try to follow along in a scratch repository rather than just watching passively.

Design Collaboration with Figma

Introduction

Figma is a browser-based design and prototyping tool that teams use to sketch ideas, build user interface screens, link those screens into clickable prototypes, and hand specifications off to developers. In this course it is the primary tool you will use for your project: you will create an education team workspace (free for students through Figma's education plan), use FigJam boards for brainstorming and affinity mapping, build UI mockups and prototypes in Figma design files, and use Dev Mode to inspect spacing, colors, and generated code snippets when you move from design to implementation.

What makes Figma especially useful here is collaboration. Multiple teammates can work in the same file at the same time, leave comments directly on a screen, and share a single link instead of emailing files back and forth. That matters for the course project, where you will be assigning team roles and iterating on designs based on feedback rather than producing one final artifact alone.

The videos below are hands-on tutorials. As you watch, focus less on memorizing menu locations (Figma's interface changes over time) and more on the workflow: how a rough wireframe becomes a styled screen, how frames are connected into a prototype that someone can actually click through, and how reusable components keep a design consistent. Try following along in your own file rather than just watching, and pause to recreate each step.

AI Tools in HCI and Development

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has moved from a research curiosity into the everyday toolkit of designers and developers. Generative models now help teams brainstorm interface concepts, draft wireframes and copy, generate and refactor code, write tests, and summarize user research. In this topic we survey where these tools actually fit in the design and development workflow, what they do well, and where human judgment still has to carry the work.

This matters for CEN3722 because your course project will run through the same lifecycle these tools touch: scoping and planning, sketching and prototyping, building, and evaluating. Knowing which tasks an AI assistant can accelerate — and which ones it tends to make worse, such as understanding real users, justifying design decisions, or ensuring accessibility — will make you a faster and more credible team member. It also sets up the course policy on responsible use: AI assistance is a tool you disclose and verify, not a substitute for your own reasoning or a source you can cite blindly.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, which stage of the workflow is each tool being applied to (ideation, visual design, coding, documentation)? Second, what verification step does the presenter take — or fail to take — after the AI produces output? Third, notice how quickly specific tools and features change; the underlying workflow patterns will outlast any particular product shown on screen.

Development Environment Setup

Introduction

Before you can design or evaluate an interface, you need a place to build it. This topic walks you through setting up the tools your team will use all semester: a code editor, Git for version control, a GitHub repository for sharing work, and GitHub Codespaces as a cloud-based option when a local setup is inconvenient or when a teammate's machine won't cooperate. You will also establish a sensible project folder structure and naming conventions, then verify that everything works end to end by making a change, committing it, and pushing it.

This matters because your course project is a team effort. Version control is what lets several people edit the same project without overwriting each other, and it gives you a history you can roll back to when an experiment goes wrong. Agreeing on a folder structure and workflow now saves hours of confusion later, and it is a skill employers assume you already have.

As you watch, focus on the sequence of actions rather than memorizing menus: initialize or clone a repository, stage changes, commit with a clear message, push, and pull. Notice where each tool overlaps (the editor's Git panel versus the command line versus the browser) and pick the path you will actually use. Pause and perform each step on your own machine or in a Codespace as you go, and finish by confirming that your commits appear on GitHub.

Team Collaboration and Project Kickoff

Introduction

Almost everything you produce in this course will be produced with other people. This topic is about setting up that collaboration deliberately rather than letting it happen by accident: forming a team, agreeing on who does what, writing it down in a team charter, and bounding the course project so the scope is something your team can actually finish in a semester.

The practices here come straight from project management. A team charter, a roles-and-responsibilities list, a communication plan, and a scope statement are all standard project documents, and the vocabulary around them (stakeholder, deliverable, milestone, scope creep) is vocabulary you will see again in industry. Learning it now makes your design work later in the course easier to coordinate and easier to explain to a client or instructor.

The three videos below come from a project management practitioner channel and cover the documents, the communication planning, and the terminology side of kickoff. As you watch, map what you hear onto your own team: which documents does our project actually need, how will we decide when and how we talk to each other, and where exactly does our project stop? Take notes you can paste directly into your charter and project plan drafts.
