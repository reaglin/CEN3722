Google Play Store Submission Process

Introduction

Publishing an Android app is not simply a matter of uploading a file. Google Play requires a registered developer account, a properly built and signed release artifact (typically an Android App Bundle), a complete store listing with text and graphic assets, declarations about what your app does with user data, and a release strategy that moves the app through internal, closed, open, and finally production tracks. Each of these steps has rules attached, and failing any one of them can delay or block a release.

In this course, the submission process matters because it is where interface design decisions meet real-world distribution constraints. Screenshots, feature graphics, short and full descriptions, and permission disclosures are all user-facing interface elements — they are the first interaction a user has with your product, and they are judged by reviewers against published policy. Understanding the pipeline also helps you plan your project: signing keys, package names, and privacy declarations are easier to get right at the start than to retrofit.

As you watch these videos, pay attention to three things: the sequence of steps in the Play Console and what information is required at each one; the difference between build-time concerns (signing, bundles, versioning) and listing-time concerns (metadata, assets, content rating); and how policy requirements — especially around permissions and user disclosure — shape what you can legitimately ask of users. Note that Play Console interfaces and policies change over time, so treat the walkthroughs as a map of the process and confirm current details against Google's official documentation.

Apple App Store Submission Process

Introduction

Publishing an app to the Apple App Store is less a single button press than a sequence of administrative, technical, and design steps. Before any code ships, a developer (or organization) must enroll in the Apple Developer Program, generate certificates and provisioning profiles so that builds are cryptographically signed, create an app record in App Store Connect, upload a build through Xcode, and supply metadata and marketing assets that meet Apple's specifications. Only then does the app enter App Review, where human reviewers check it against the App Store Review Guidelines.

This matters in a human-computer interfaces course because Apple's requirements are, in large part, interface requirements. Screenshot dimensions, app icons, privacy disclosures, age ratings, and accessibility information are all part of how users encounter and judge your product before they ever launch it. Many common rejections — broken links, placeholder content, misleading screenshots, missing privacy details, poor usability on certain devices — are failures of interface preparation rather than failures of engineering.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the order of operations: what has to exist in App Store Connect before a build can be uploaded, what Xcode handles automatically versus what you must configure, and where beta testing (TestFlight) fits relative to final review submission. Note also every place a video mentions an asset, a description field, or a compliance question, and start a checklist you can later apply to your own interface project.

Microsoft Store Submission Process

Introduction

Publishing a Windows application to the Microsoft Store is a structured process with four main stages: registering a developer account in Microsoft Partner Center, packaging your app (most commonly as an MSIX package), building a store listing with the required metadata and visual assets, and passing certification against Microsoft Store policies. Each stage has concrete requirements — an identity-verified account, a signed and correctly manifested package, screenshots and descriptions at specified sizes and lengths, and content that complies with the Store Policies and Developer Agreement.

This matters in an HCI course because the store listing is often the first interface a user encounters. Your icons, screenshots, short description, and accessibility information shape whether someone installs your app at all, and store requirements effectively impose a baseline of usability, accessibility, and transparency on the product itself. Learning the Microsoft process also gives you a point of comparison with the Apple App Store and Google Play, which solve the same problems with different rules.

As you watch the videos below, note that they come from Microsoft's own developer conference sessions, so they present the publishing pipeline from the platform owner's perspective. Look for the sequence of steps from code to published app, the specific listing elements that influence discoverability and conversion, and the policy and business-model changes that define the current Store. Keep in mind that these sessions are a few years old: always confirm exact size limits, fees, and policy clauses against current Microsoft Partner Center documentation before you submit.

Web Deployment Strategies

Introduction

Not every interface you build will be distributed through an app store. For web applications, "publication" means deploying your code to a server that the public can reach, attaching a domain name to it, securing it with HTTPS, and keeping it updated as you continue to make changes. This topic walks through the practical choices involved: where to host (managed platforms, static site hosts, or your own virtual private server), how to automate builds and deployments, how domains and SSL certificates are configured, what it takes to ship a Progressive Web App, and how performance and SEO affect whether people actually find and stay on your site.

These concerns matter in this course because the usability work you do is only visible to users once the project is live, fast, and trustworthy. A slow first load, a browser warning about an insecure connection, or a broken deploy undoes a lot of careful interface design. Deployment also changes how you work: once a build pipeline is in place, pushing a change to a repository can automatically test and publish it, which shortens the loop between user feedback and the fix.

The three videos below demonstrate three different points on the hosting spectrum, from fully managed to fully self-administered. While watching, pay attention to what each platform does for you automatically versus what you must configure yourself — especially the steps around connecting a Git repository, setting a build command, pointing a custom domain, and provisioning a certificate. Also notice how each workflow would handle a second, third, and fiftieth deployment, since that repeatability is the real point of continuous deployment.

Asset Requirements and Store Listing Optimization

Introduction

Building a working app is only half the job of shipping it. Before anything reaches a user, both the Apple App Store and Google Play require a package of graphical and textual assets: an icon in specific sizes, screenshots sized for multiple devices, an optional preview video, a title and subtitle, a short and long description, keyword or category metadata, a content rating, and a link to a privacy policy. These assets are not decoration — they are the interface between your product and a person deciding, in a few seconds, whether to install it.

This topic treats the store listing as a designed interface in its own right. The same HCI principles you have applied to screens apply here: visual consistency and branding in the icon, legibility and information hierarchy in screenshot captions, honest and accessible descriptions, and clear disclosure of what data you collect. App Store Optimization (ASO) is the practice of tuning the searchable and persuasive parts of that listing so the right users find your app and choose to install it.

The three videos below approach ASO from different angles: a conceptual introduction, a strategy overview from a major mobile marketing company, and a hands-on walkthrough using an ASO tool. As you watch, note the recurring vocabulary — keywords, conversion rate, impressions, installs — and pay attention to the distinction between the metadata fields that are indexed for search and the visual assets that drive conversion. Also notice where platform differences between Apple and Google are mentioned, since those differences will shape how you prepare your own submission assets.

Build Finalization and Submission Readiness

Introduction

Getting an app from "it works on my machine" to "it is live in a store" involves a set of concrete, checkable steps. A release build is not simply a debug build with a different name: it typically uses different signing credentials, different configuration values, optimization and shrinking settings, and often a different distribution format (for example an Android App Bundle rather than a plain APK). Before that build goes anywhere, it needs to be exercised against a pre-submission checklist — does it install cleanly on a fresh device, do permissions and accounts behave, do crash reports and analytics point at production endpoints, and does the interface still meet the usability and accessibility standards you have been applying all term?

Alongside the binary itself, stores expect a package of deliverables: a correctly incremented version number and build number, release notes that describe what changed, store listing metadata, screenshots, and any declarations the platform requires. Sloppy version numbering is one of the most common reasons an upload is rejected outright, and vague release notes slow down human review. Treating these artifacts as part of the deliverable — not as paperwork added at the last minute — is what separates a submission that moves through review from one that bounces back.

The three videos below approach this from different angles: build configuration on native Android, build configuration in a cross-platform workflow, and the tooling a store itself offers for testing and submission. While watching, pay attention to the distinction between debug and release configuration, to where version and build identifiers are declared, and to what each platform asks you to supply before it will accept an upload. Note also how much of this work can be automated once, then repeated for every release.
