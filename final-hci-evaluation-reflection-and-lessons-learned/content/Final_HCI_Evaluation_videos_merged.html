Connecting HCI Theory to Design Outcomes

Introduction

Throughout this course you have made dozens of design decisions: where to put a button, how to label it, what feedback to show after a click. This topic asks you to look back at those decisions and name the theory behind them. Concepts such as affordances, the human-computer interaction cycle, and direct manipulation are not just vocabulary for an exam — they are the reasoning you use to explain why an interface works, and the evidence you cite when someone challenges your design.

Connecting theory to outcomes runs in two directions. Forward, theory guides choices before you build: a model of how users perceive, act, and interpret feedback tells you what to prototype in the first place. Backward, theory gives you a yardstick for evaluation: you can inspect your finished product and ask whether its controls actually signal their use, whether feedback closes the loop of interaction, and whether users manipulate meaningful objects or wrestle with hidden commands.

The three short videos below revisit core concepts at a practical level. As you watch, keep your own project on screen or in mind. For each idea presented, try to find one place in your interface where you applied it well, and one place where you did not — those paired examples are exactly the material you need for your final reflection, your design rationale presentation, and your list of proposed improvements.

Review of the Analysis Phase

Introduction

Before you can judge whether your final interface succeeded, you have to revisit where it started. The analysis phase is the stretch of work that happens before any screens are drawn: talking to users, observing how they currently accomplish their goals, breaking those goals into tasks, and translating all of it into a set of requirements with a realistic scope. Decisions made here quietly determine almost everything that follows — which features exist, which users are served well, and which usability problems were avoidable from the beginning.

In this module you are reflecting rather than starting fresh, so the purpose of reviewing analysis is diagnostic. Which research method did you choose, and did it actually surface the information you needed? Did your task analysis capture real workflows, or an idealized version of them? Were your requirements specific enough to test against later, and did scope creep or scope gaps show up in the final evaluation? These questions give you the evidence you will need when you defend design decisions and propose improvements.

The three videos below revisit the fundamentals of qualitative user research, the overall shape of the UX process, and the range of research methods available. As you watch, resist treating them as introductory review. Instead, compare each practice described to what you actually did on your project, and note specific gaps you can name and explain in your reflection.

Review of the Prototyping Phase

Introduction

Prototyping is the bridge between an idea and a working interface. In this topic you look back at the prototyping phase of your project and examine how low-fidelity artifacts such as sketches and wireframes helped you generate and compare design alternatives quickly, and how high-fidelity prototypes added visual detail, realistic interaction, and a closer approximation of the final product. The distinction matters because fidelity is a deliberate choice: low fidelity invites criticism and change, while high fidelity invites detailed feedback on layout, typography, color, and flow.

This review also focuses on iteration. Prototypes are rarely right the first time, and the value of the method comes from cycling between versions as user feedback, usability testing, and accessibility checks reveal problems. Being able to trace how a screen changed from sketch to wireframe to interactive prototype is exactly the kind of evidence you will need when you present and defend your design decisions at the end of the course.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: what level of detail is appropriate at each stage, what kind of feedback each fidelity level can realistically produce, and how the designer decides what to change between versions. Try to map each step shown back to a decision you actually made in your own project, including the ones you would make differently now.

Review of the Evaluation Phase

Introduction

The evaluation phase is where design claims meet evidence. Across this course you have moved from user research and requirements through sketches, prototypes, and iteration; now you look back and ask what your evaluation activities actually told you, how trustworthy that information was, and whether the changes you made in response were the right ones. Reviewing the evaluation phase means being honest about method choice (usability testing with real users, heuristic or expert inspection, or a combination), about the quality and quantity of feedback you gathered, and about the gap between what you intended to measure and what you could measure in the time available.

This review also feeds directly into your final deliverable. When you present your project, you will be expected to justify design decisions with evaluation data rather than preference, to show how specific findings became specific interface changes, and to state plainly which problems remain unresolved. That requires a shared vocabulary: what usability actually means as a measurable quality, how heuristic evaluation is performed in a disciplined way, and how findings are prioritized so that limited effort goes to the issues that matter most.

The three short videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-standing source of practitioner guidance on usability. As you watch, map each idea back to your own project: which usability components did you actually test, which heuristics would have caught the problems your users hit, and how would you assign severity to the issues still sitting in your backlog. Take notes you can reuse in your reflection and final presentation.

Structured Feedback on Design Quality

Introduction

Near the end of a design project, the most valuable input you receive is rarely "I like it" or "I don't like it." It is structured feedback: comments tied to specific dimensions of design quality such as usability, learnability, consistency, visual hierarchy, accessibility, and fit with the users' actual tasks. This topic looks at how to frame a critique so that it produces that kind of comment, how to deliver criticism that a teammate can act on, and how to receive criticism without becoming defensive or rewriting your design in response to every stray opinion.

Structured feedback also serves a second purpose in this course. When several reviewers independently flag the same screen, the same control, or the same confusing label, you are no longer looking at one person's taste; you are looking at a pattern. Those patterns are what you carry into your final reflection and your list of proposed improvements, and they are the evidence you will cite when you defend your design decisions to an audience.

The three videos below come from the Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established usability consultancy. As you watch, pay attention to the mechanics: how a critique session is set up before anyone speaks, what makes a comment specific enough to act on, and how a quality dimension like accessibility can be defined precisely enough to be reviewed rather than vaguely praised. Then apply the same mechanics to your own project review.

Process Execution and Team Collaboration

Introduction

Designing an interface is only half the work; the other half is managing the process and the people who carry it out. This topic asks you to look back at how your team actually operated over the semester: how you planned and sequenced design activities, how work was divided among members, how you communicated and handled disagreement, and which tools and documents kept everyone aligned. These are the factors that most often explain why a project ran smoothly or fell behind, and they are rarely visible in the finished product alone.

In this course, honest reflection on process is part of your final evaluation. Employers and clients care not just about whether your prototype works, but about whether you can diagnose what went wrong, credit what went right, and describe how you would run the next project differently. A clear account of role distribution, meeting rhythm, conflict resolution, and documentation practice makes your final presentation far more credible than vague claims that "the team worked well together."

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established UX research and consulting firm. As you watch, map what you hear onto your own experience: Which phase of team development did your group get stuck in? Which workshop activities would have helped you converge on decisions faster? Where did stakeholder or instructor feedback get lost because no one documented it? Take notes you can quote directly in your reflection.

Reflection and Lessons Learned

Introduction

Reflection is the step that turns a finished project into durable learning. Now that your interface has been built, evaluated, and presented, this topic asks you to look backward with discipline: what design decisions held up under user testing, which assumptions about your users were wrong, where did ergonomic, psychological, cultural, or accessibility considerations surprise you, and what would you do differently if you started the project again tomorrow? Reflection here is not a vague feelings exercise — it is a structured, evidence-based critique of your own process.

The topic has two levels. Individual reflective practice asks you to write honestly about your own contributions, skills, and blind spots, supported by concrete evidence from your design artifacts and evaluation data. Team-level lessons learned asks the group to meet, surface what worked and what did not without blaming individuals, and record outcomes that a future team could actually act on. Both levels should connect explicitly back to course concepts — usability heuristics, user-centered design, accessibility standards, cognitive load, and the evaluation methods you used.

As you watch, look for structure: how a reflective statement moves from description to analysis to action, how a lessons-learned meeting is facilitated so that criticism stays constructive, and how reflection is built into project-based HCI work rather than tacked on at the end. Your goal is to come away with a small number of specific, actionable improvements you could carry into your next design project.
