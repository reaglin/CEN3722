Introduction

Prototyping is the bridge between an idea and a working interface. In this topic you look back at the prototyping phase of your project and examine how low-fidelity artifacts such as sketches and wireframes helped you generate and compare design alternatives quickly, and how high-fidelity prototypes added visual detail, realistic interaction, and a closer approximation of the final product. The distinction matters because fidelity is a deliberate choice: low fidelity invites criticism and change, while high fidelity invites detailed feedback on layout, typography, color, and flow.

This review also focuses on iteration. Prototypes are rarely right the first time, and the value of the method comes from cycling between versions as user feedback, usability testing, and accessibility checks reveal problems. Being able to trace how a screen changed from sketch to wireframe to interactive prototype is exactly the kind of evidence you will need when you present and defend your design decisions at the end of the course.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: what level of detail is appropriate at each stage, what kind of feedback each fidelity level can realistically produce, and how the designer decides what to change between versions. Try to map each step shown back to a decision you actually made in your own project, including the ones you would make differently now.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Reviewing these videos helps you reconstruct and critique your own prototyping history, which is the foundation for this module's work. The wireframing and high-fidelity videos give you vocabulary and criteria for evaluating your final interface against the requirements and usability goals you set early on (MO1) and for explaining, in a presentation, why a design changed between versions (MO2). Seeing the standard progression from sketch to styled, interactive prototype also supports honest reflection on where ergonomic, psychological, cultural, or accessibility concerns were caught late or missed entirely (MO3). Finally, understanding prototyping as an ongoing cycle rather than a finished step makes it natural to propose a next iteration that addresses unresolved usability issues (MO4).
