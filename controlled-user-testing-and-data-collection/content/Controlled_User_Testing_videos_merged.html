Foundations of Experimental Design for Usability Testing

Introduction

Controlled usability testing borrows its logic from experimental science. Before you can say that one interface is "better" than another, you need a plan that specifies what you are changing, what you are measuring, and what you are holding constant. That plan is your experimental design, and it determines whether the numbers and observations you collect actually mean anything.

This topic introduces the vocabulary and decisions behind that plan: independent variables (what you manipulate, such as two versions of a navigation menu), dependent variables (what you measure, such as time-on-task, error counts, or completion rates), and the control of everything else so that differences in results can be traced back to the design and not to noise. You will also look at internal validity (did my manipulation really cause the observed effect?) versus external validity (will this hold up outside my lab?), the difference between formative studies that shape a design in progress and summative studies that evaluate a finished one, and the ethical obligations you owe participants: informed consent, the right to stop, confidentiality, and never making a participant feel that they are the thing being tested.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running list of three things: what was manipulated, what was measured, and what could have gone wrong (a confound, a biased task order, a leading question). Notice also where the videos emphasize structured quantitative comparison versus open-ended qualitative insight. Both are legitimate; the choice depends on the question you are asking and on where you are in the design cycle.

Participant Selection and Recruitment

Introduction

Any usability study is only as good as the people who sit down to use the interface. Participant selection and recruitment is the process of deciding who your target users actually are, writing down the criteria that qualify or disqualify someone from your study, finding those people, confirming they really match your criteria, and getting them scheduled into sessions. Get this wrong and even a perfectly run test session produces findings that do not describe your real users.

In this module you will be running controlled test sessions and collecting measurable data, so recruitment directly determines whether that data means anything. A sample drawn from convenient friends and classmates, or from people who misrepresented their experience on a screener, introduces bias that cannot be corrected afterward. Sample size matters too: the number of participants you need depends on whether you are looking for qualitative insight into problems or quantitative estimates of metrics such as task time and completion rate.

As you watch the three videos below, keep a running list of practical decisions you will have to make for your own study: how you would word a screener question so it does not telegraph the "right" answer, which recruiting channel fits your budget and timeline, what you would exclude on, and how many participants your study goal actually justifies. All three come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established usability research and consulting firm, so expect practitioner-oriented guidance rather than academic theory.

Task Scenario Scripting

Introduction

A usability test is only as good as the tasks you ask people to do. A task scenario is a short, realistic situation that gives the participant a reason to use the interface — for example, "You want to meet a friend downtown on Friday night; find a restaurant that takes reservations and book a table for two" — rather than a direct instruction like "click the reservations button." Good scenarios supply just enough context to make behavior natural, avoid giving away the interface vocabulary or the navigation path, and end in a state you can actually observe and score.

This topic covers how to write those scenarios and assemble them into a test script. You will look at what separates a strong scenario from a leading or vague one, how to phrase context and prompts, how to attach measurable success criteria so you can later report completion rates, error counts, and time-on-task, how to pilot a script and revise wording that confuses participants, and how to sequence tasks so that earlier tasks do not teach participants the answers to later ones. The script also includes the non-task parts of the session: the introduction, consent, think-aloud instructions, and transitions.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: the exact wording used in example tasks (what is included, what is deliberately left out), how each speaker defines "done" for a task, and how the script is used as a facilitation tool during the session rather than just a planning document. Notice also how much preparation happens before a participant ever arrives.

Think-Aloud Protocol

Introduction

The think-aloud protocol asks participants to verbalize their thoughts while they work through tasks with an interface. Instead of guessing why a user hesitated, clicked the wrong button, or abandoned a flow, you hear their reasoning in the moment: what they expected, what label confused them, what they thought would happen next. The method rests on the idea that people can report the contents of their working memory as they perform a task, which makes it one of the richest sources of qualitative usability data available in a controlled test.

In this module you are learning to run a controlled session and capture clean data, so think-aloud is both a skill and a risk. Done well, it surfaces the reasoning behind errors and slowdowns. Done poorly, it biases behavior: leading prompts put words in the participant's mouth, over-talking slows the user and distorts time-on-task, and some tasks (highly practiced expert work, for example) simply break down when narrated. That is why the distinction between concurrent think-aloud (speaking while doing) and retrospective think-aloud (reviewing afterward, often with a screen recording) matters so much.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: the exact wording facilitators use to instruct and re-prompt participants, the moments where the facilitator deliberately stays quiet, and the signals that tell you concurrent think-aloud is not working and a different approach is needed. Also notice how session logistics -- consent, recording setup, note-taking, timing -- are built into the routine rather than improvised.

Quantitative Data Collection

Introduction

Quantitative data collection is the part of usability testing where you turn what happens in a session into numbers you can compare, report, and defend. The core metrics are familiar ones: whether a participant completed the task, how long it took, how many errors they made, and how difficult they said it felt. On top of these behavioral measures sit standardized attitudinal instruments such as the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the Single Ease Question (SEQ), which have been used widely enough that your scores can be compared against established benchmarks.

What makes these numbers trustworthy is not the arithmetic but the discipline behind it. You need a data collection instrument prepared in advance (a scoring sheet or form with explicit definitions of success, error, and start/stop points for timing), consistent administration across every participant, and a clear plan for how individual scores will be aggregated into summary values. Without that consistency, two observers watching the same session can record different results, and the resulting averages are meaningless.

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group and cover the standardized questionnaires most often used in practice, how to choose between them, and the important caution about collecting metrics in small qualitative studies. While watching, pay attention to exactly when each instrument is administered, how scores are calculated and interpreted, and what sample size a metric realistically requires before you should treat it as a number rather than as a story about one user.

Qualitative Data Collection

Introduction

Qualitative data is everything you capture during a usability test that is not a number: what participants say as they think aloud, where they hesitate, the workarounds they invent, the confused facial expression before a misclick, and the explanations they give in a post-test interview. Where quantitative measures tell you that a task took 94 seconds or failed 4 times out of 10, qualitative data tells you why it happened and what the user believed was going on. Both are needed; neither is enough alone.

In this topic we look at how to capture that non-numerical evidence in a disciplined way. Structured observation and note-taking keep your records consistent across participants instead of dependent on memory. Post-test interviews and debriefs let you probe moments you noticed without leading the participant. Verbatim quotes and critical incidents (the specific breakdowns, errors, and moments of delight) become the evidence you cite in a findings report. Affinity diagramming is then the synthesis step: clustering dozens of raw observations into themes so that patterns, not anecdotes, drive your recommendations.

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-standing UX research practice. They do not walk through a test session start to finish; instead they address the synthesis and interpretation side of qualitative work — how to organize what users say, think, feel, and do into a shared artifact, and how to classify the problems you observe. While watching, pay attention to the vocabulary used for categorizing raw observations, and think about how each technique would change the notes you take during a session. Ask yourself: what would I have to write down in the moment for this analysis method to work afterward?

Conducting Live User Testing Sessions

Introduction

Running a usability test is part research method and part live event management. Once participants are scheduled and tasks are written, your team still has to decide who facilitates, who takes notes, who handles recording and timing, how the room (or video call) is arranged, what you say in the first two minutes, and what you do when something goes wrong mid-session. This topic covers those practical logistics and the interpersonal dynamics that surround them.

These skills matter in this course because the quality of your data depends heavily on how the session is run. A facilitator who nudges a participant toward the right button, a noisy observer who reacts to a failure, or a missing consent step can all cost you an entire session's worth of usable results. Clear team roles, a stable environment, a consistent opening script, and a disciplined debrief are what make results from different participants comparable.

As you watch, pay attention to sequence and wording: what gets done before the participant arrives, the exact phrasing used to set expectations, how facilitators stay neutral when a participant struggles or asks for help, and what happens at the close of a session. Note where each video gives you something you could literally copy into your team's checklist or facilitator script for your own project sessions.

Compiling Structured Results for Analysis

Introduction

Once a usability test session ends, you are left with a messy pile of evidence: session notes, observer tallies, recordings, think-aloud quotes, task timings, and error counts. Compiling structured results is the work of turning that raw material into something a design team can act on. It involves organizing data by participant and by task, grouping individual observations into recurring usability problems, rating those problems by severity, and then assembling a report that pairs numbers with illustrative evidence and concrete recommendations.

This matters in CEN3722 because a usability test only has value if its findings reach the people who build the interface in a form they can use. A report that lists forty unsorted observations with no prioritization gives a team no idea where to start. A report that states results more confidently than the data warrants can push a team toward the wrong redesign. Your job is to be both useful and honest: clear about what you found, clear about how strong the evidence is, and clear about what should change.

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established usability consultancy. As you watch, focus on three things: how severity criteria help you decide what to report first, how to describe findings truthfully when sample sizes are small, and how framing and context affect whether your audience actually engages with your recommendations. Take notes you can apply directly to your team's results document.
