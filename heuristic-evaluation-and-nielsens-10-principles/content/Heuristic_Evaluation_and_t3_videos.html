Introduction

Heuristic evaluation (HE) is a discount usability inspection method: a small group of evaluators independently inspects an interface against a set of recognized usability principles, then their findings are pooled into a single list of problems. Knowing the ten heuristics is only half the skill — the other half is running the evaluation properly, which is what this topic covers.

The process has distinct phases. Preparation means defining the scope of the interface, choosing representative tasks or screens, and briefing evaluators on the heuristic set and on any domain knowledge they need. The individual evaluation phase means each evaluator passes through the interface alone (usually at least twice — once to get a feel for the flow, once to inspect elements closely) so that no one's judgment is anchored by someone else's. Documentation means recording each problem separately, naming the heuristic it violates, pointing to the exact location in the interface, and rating severity. Finally, the findings from all evaluators are consolidated; Nielsen's well-known guidance is that a single evaluator finds only a fraction of the problems, and that roughly three to five evaluators give the best return on effort.

As you watch, pay attention to procedure rather than to the heuristics themselves: when do evaluators work alone versus together, how is a problem written up so someone else can act on it, and what makes a finding specific enough to be useful? Notice also how severity ratings and recommendations are attached to problems, since you will be asked to produce both in this module's assignment.
