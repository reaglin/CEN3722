Introduction

A heuristic evaluation is only as useful as the report that comes out of it. Once you have inspected an interface and identified violations of Nielsen's 10 principles, you have to turn a pile of observations into a document that a design team, a product manager, or a client can actually act on. That document typically includes an executive summary, a description of scope and method (what was evaluated, by whom, under what conditions), a prioritized list of findings, and an appendix of supporting evidence.

The craft is in the findings themselves. A strong problem description names the specific screen or element, states the heuristic violated, explains the likely consequence for the user, and assigns a severity rating — all without blaming the designer or wandering into speculation. A strong recommendation is concrete enough to be implemented and is clearly separated from the finding, so stakeholders can see the problem even if they choose a different solution. Screenshots with callouts, annotated flows, and short illustrative quotes or scenarios make findings credible and quick to grasp.

The videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group. As you watch, pay attention to three things: how findings are organized and prioritized for an audience, how to represent evidence honestly rather than overstating what a small inspection can prove, and how supporting material such as quotes or visuals is edited and framed so it informs rather than misleads.
