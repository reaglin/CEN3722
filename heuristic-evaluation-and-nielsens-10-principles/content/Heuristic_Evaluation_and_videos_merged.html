Introduction to Heuristic Evaluation

Introduction

Heuristic evaluation (HE) is a usability inspection method: instead of recruiting end users, a small group of evaluators inspects an interface on their own and judges it against a short list of recognized usability principles. The method was popularized by Jakob Nielsen and Rolf Molich in the early 1990s, and it remains one of the fastest, cheapest ways to surface usability problems in a design before (or alongside) user testing. In this course it serves as your bridge from abstract design principles to concrete, defensible critique of real interfaces.

The subtopics here frame four questions. What is HE, procedurally — who evaluates, how many evaluators, how findings are merged? Where did it come from, and how does it relate to other inspection methods such as cognitive walkthroughs? When should you reach for it — early in design, between usability tests, or as a quick audit of an existing product? And what are its limits — evaluators are not users, findings can be subjective, and HE tends to catch surface-level problems more reliably than deep task or workflow failures.

As you watch, pay attention to process details you will need to reproduce in your own evaluation: evaluating independently before discussing, documenting each issue with the heuristic it violates and where it occurs, consolidating duplicate findings across evaluators, and assigning severity. Also note where each video says HE should not stand alone — that caveat is part of understanding the method, not a footnote to it.

Nielsen's Ten Usability Heuristics

Introduction

Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics are the most widely used set of general principles for interface design. They are deliberately broad "rules of thumb" rather than strict guidelines, which is what makes them useful across websites, mobile apps, desktop software, and even physical devices. In this topic you will work through each heuristic in turn: visibility of system status, match between the system and the real world, user control and freedom, consistency and standards, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, flexibility and efficiency of use, aesthetic and minimalist design, helping users recognize and recover from errors, and help and documentation.

These principles matter in this course because they are the vocabulary of heuristic evaluation. When you inspect an interface, you are not simply saying "this feels bad" — you are naming the specific principle that is violated, explaining why it causes a problem for users, and proposing a concrete fix. Being able to recall all ten heuristics and recognize them in the wild is the prerequisite for everything that follows in this module, including severity rating and recommendation writing.

As you watch, keep two questions in mind for each heuristic: what user problem does this principle prevent, and what does a violation actually look like in a real interface? Try to collect at least one positive example and one violation for each of the ten. Also notice that heuristics sometimes pull against each other — a minimalist design may hide status information, and an innovative pattern may break consistency — so part of your job as an evaluator is judgment, not just pattern matching.

The Heuristic Evaluation Process

Introduction

Heuristic evaluation (HE) is a discount usability inspection method: a small group of evaluators independently inspects an interface against a set of recognized usability principles, then their findings are pooled into a single list of problems. Knowing the ten heuristics is only half the skill — the other half is running the evaluation properly, which is what this topic covers.

The process has distinct phases. Preparation means defining the scope of the interface, choosing representative tasks or screens, and briefing evaluators on the heuristic set and on any domain knowledge they need. The individual evaluation phase means each evaluator passes through the interface alone (usually at least twice — once to get a feel for the flow, once to inspect elements closely) so that no one's judgment is anchored by someone else's. Documentation means recording each problem separately, naming the heuristic it violates, pointing to the exact location in the interface, and rating severity. Finally, the findings from all evaluators are consolidated; Nielsen's well-known guidance is that a single evaluator finds only a fraction of the problems, and that roughly three to five evaluators give the best return on effort.

As you watch, pay attention to procedure rather than to the heuristics themselves: when do evaluators work alone versus together, how is a problem written up so someone else can act on it, and what makes a finding specific enough to be useful? Notice also how severity ratings and recommendations are attached to problems, since you will be asked to produce both in this module's assignment.

Severity Rating

Introduction

Finding usability problems is only half the work of a heuristic evaluation. Once you have a list of heuristic violations, someone has to decide which ones get fixed first, because development teams rarely have time to address everything. Severity ratings are the tool evaluators use to communicate how much each problem matters, turning a long, flat list of issues into a prioritized set of recommendations.

Nielsen's severity scale runs from 0 (not a usability problem at all) to 4 (usability catastrophe that must be fixed before release), with cosmetic, minor, and major problems in between. The rating you assign is a judgment call, but it is not arbitrary: it is based on how frequently users will encounter the problem, how much trouble it causes them when they do, and whether users can learn to work around it or are blocked by it every time. Because judgment varies between people, evaluations usually combine ratings from several evaluators and record each issue with its heuristic, its severity, and a suggested fix.

As you watch these videos, pay attention to the criteria the speakers use to justify a rating rather than just the numbers themselves. Notice how severity is described in terms of user impact, and think about how you would document a rating so that a teammate reading your report could understand why you chose it. This habit of recording and defending ratings is exactly what you will be asked to do in your own evaluation assignments.

Aggregating Findings Across Evaluators

Introduction

Heuristic evaluation is deliberately done by several evaluators working independently, because any single person catches only a fraction of the usability problems in an interface. That design choice creates a follow-up task: once everyone has finished inspecting on their own, the separate lists of findings have to be combined into one clean, agreed-upon set of problems that a team can actually act on. That combining step is called aggregation.

Aggregation usually happens in a structured meeting. Evaluators walk through their findings one at a time, merge duplicates that were described in different words, decide whether two similar observations are really one underlying problem or two distinct ones, settle which heuristic each violation maps to, and negotiate a single severity rating when individual ratings disagree. The output is a deduplicated, prioritized problem list with enough detail (location, heuristic violated, severity, suggested fix) for designers to use.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to two things. First, how the evaluation process is structured from the start, since consistent note-taking and clear problem descriptions during individual evaluation are what make deduplication possible later. Second, how small numbers of evaluators or test users can still produce trustworthy, decision-ready conclusions — the same logic that justifies why we aggregate across a handful of evaluators rather than relying on one.

Writing Formal Heuristic Evaluation Reports

Introduction

A heuristic evaluation is only as useful as the report that comes out of it. Once you have inspected an interface and identified violations of Nielsen's 10 principles, you have to turn a pile of observations into a document that a design team, a product manager, or a client can actually act on. That document typically includes an executive summary, a description of scope and method (what was evaluated, by whom, under what conditions), a prioritized list of findings, and an appendix of supporting evidence.

The craft is in the findings themselves. A strong problem description names the specific screen or element, states the heuristic violated, explains the likely consequence for the user, and assigns a severity rating — all without blaming the designer or wandering into speculation. A strong recommendation is concrete enough to be implemented and is clearly separated from the finding, so stakeholders can see the problem even if they choose a different solution. Screenshots with callouts, annotated flows, and short illustrative quotes or scenarios make findings credible and quick to grasp.

The videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group. As you watch, pay attention to three things: how findings are organized and prioritized for an audience, how to represent evidence honestly rather than overstating what a small inspection can prove, and how supporting material such as quotes or visuals is edited and framed so it informs rather than misleads.

Evaluating Peer Prototypes

Introduction

In this topic you move from learning heuristic evaluation (HE) to performing it on real work produced by your classmates. The module assignment asks you to independently evaluate three peer prototypes, applying the full HE process to each: inspect the interface, identify usability problems, classify each problem under one of Nielsen's 10 heuristics, assign a severity rating, and recommend a concrete fix. The key word is independently — in a proper heuristic evaluation, each evaluator works alone first, and findings are only pooled afterward. Comparing notes with a peer before you finish contaminates your results and robs the designer of a genuinely independent perspective.

Two other expectations shape this assignment. The first is professional conduct: you are critiquing an interface, not a person. Write findings that are specific, evidence-based, and actionable ("the Save button gives no confirmation, violating Visibility of System Status") rather than vague or personal ("this screen is bad"). The second is calibration. Because you are reviewing three different prototypes, your severity ratings should mean the same thing across all three. A severity 3 on prototype A should represent roughly the same user impact as a severity 3 on prototype C, which usually means revisiting your earlier ratings once you have seen all three.

The two videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, the organization that originated the method. As you watch the first, pay attention to the step-by-step workflow — especially the practice of evaluating alone and merging findings later, and how problems are tied to specific heuristics. The second video is about facilitating usability tests rather than HE, but watch it for tone: how an experienced professional frames a session, sets expectations, and makes clear that the interface is under scrutiny, not the person. That framing is exactly the attitude to bring to peer evaluation.
