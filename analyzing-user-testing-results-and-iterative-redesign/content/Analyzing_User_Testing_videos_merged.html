Foundations of Usability Test Data

Introduction

Every usability test produces evidence, but not all evidence looks the same. Some of it is numeric — how many participants finished a task, how long they took, how many errors they made, how they rated the system at the end. Some of it is observational — what a participant said while hesitating, where they looked for a button that wasn't there, what assumption led them down the wrong path. This topic builds the shared vocabulary you need to talk about both kinds of data and to know which kind answers which question.

The distinction matters because the two types of data do different jobs. Quantitative measures such as task completion rate, error rate, time on task, and satisfaction scores let you compare designs, track progress across iterations, and describe the size of a problem. Qualitative findings explain why the numbers look the way they do and point toward a fix. In this module you will need both: numbers to prioritize and justify, observations to diagnose and redesign.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things. First, notice when a speaker is talking about a small qualitative study versus a larger measurement study, since the sample size changes what a number is allowed to mean. Second, note the exact definition of each metric — what counts as "complete," what counts as an "error" — because sloppy definitions produce misleading results. Third, watch how satisfaction is treated as its own dimension, separate from whether a user actually succeeded.

Collecting and Organizing Test Data

Introduction

Usability testing produces a flood of raw material: observation notes, screen and audio recordings, task success and failure records, time-on-task numbers, quotes, and your own impressions. Collecting and organizing test data is the discipline of capturing that material consistently during sessions and then structuring it so that patterns across participants become visible. Without a plan for recording and organizing, findings tend to collapse into anecdotes about whichever participant was most memorable.

This topic covers four practical pieces. Data collection methods address what you capture and how (note-taking templates, recordings, observer roles, consistent prompts and session openings). Affinity diagramming is the technique of writing individual observations on separate notes and clustering them until themes emerge from the bottom up. Usability issue logs give each problem a stable identifier, description, location in the interface, affected participants, and a severity rating. Synthesizing across participants is the step where you count how many people hit the same obstacle and decide what the evidence actually supports.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to the habits that make later analysis possible: scripting the parts of a session that should be identical for every participant, using a small and explicit set of criteria so severity ratings stay consistent, and describing findings in terms that match the strength of the underlying data. These are the same habits that will let you defend your redesign priorities in this course's project work.

Interpreting Error Analysis

Introduction

When a usability test session ends, you are left with a pile of observations: a participant clicked the wrong button, backtracked three screens, typed a date in the wrong format, or quietly gave up. Error analysis is the discipline of turning those raw observations into a diagnosis. Instead of reporting "the user got confused," you classify what kind of error occurred (a slip, a mistake, a mode error, a data-entry error), trace it back to a root cause, and name the specific interface element — a label, an affordance, a layout, a feedback message — that produced it.

This matters in CEN3722 because redesign decisions are only as good as the diagnosis behind them. Two errors that look identical on a screen recording can have completely different causes: one may be a momentary slip by an otherwise capable user, while the other reveals a fundamental mismatch between the user's mental model and the system's conceptual model. Those two findings call for very different fixes. You also need a defensible way to separate critical errors (data loss, task failure, security or safety consequences) from minor ones (a brief hesitation, an easily recovered misclick), because you will never have time to fix everything.

The videos below approach this from three angles: the metrics you use to quantify errors and task performance, the research methods that generate error data in the first place, and a research-level look at how touch-based errors are captured and investigated on real devices. As you watch, keep asking three questions: How would I count and record this kind of error? What would I need to observe to infer its cause rather than just its symptom? And how would I decide whether this error is worth a redesign cycle?

Prioritizing Usability Improvements

Introduction

Usability testing almost always produces more problems than you have time or budget to fix. The real skill is deciding what to fix first. Prioritizing usability improvements means taking a raw list of findings and turning it into an ordered, defensible plan: which issues block users outright, which merely annoy them, which are cheap to repair, and which require architectural change.

This topic introduces four related tools. Severity rating frameworks give you a consistent scale (typically combining how badly the problem hurts the user, how often it occurs, and how persistent it is). Impact versus effort mapping plots findings on a simple grid so quick wins and expensive long shots become visible. Aligning priorities with design goals keeps the ranking honest, so that you fix what matters for the product's purpose and target users rather than what is easiest to argue about. Finally, communicating priorities to stakeholders is what makes the whole exercise useful, since prioritization only pays off if developers, product owners, and clients accept the ordering.

As you watch the videos, pay attention to the specific criteria each method uses, and notice how a judgment call ("this feels bad") gets converted into something comparable and reportable. Ask yourself how you would apply each framework to the findings from your own test sessions, and what evidence you would cite when someone pushes back on your ranking.

Principles of Iterative Redesign

Introduction

Iterative redesign is the engine of user-centered design. Rather than treating a design as something you get right once, iterative practice treats every version as a hypothesis: you build it, put it in front of users, learn where it fails, change it, and test again. Each pass through this cycle should be smaller, more focused, and better informed than the one before it.

In this module you are working with real testing results, so the key question becomes what to do with them. Some findings call for targeted refinement — a label, a control, a step in a flow — while others reveal that the underlying concept or structure is wrong and a wholesale rethink is cheaper than patching. Deciding which situation you are in, recording why you made the change (your design rationale), and defining in advance what "better" would look like in the next round are the skills this topic develops.

As you watch, look for three things: how each speaker describes the loop itself (what happens at each stage and what triggers another pass), how they distinguish refining an existing idea from exploring alternative ideas, and how testing is positioned as a source of evidence rather than opinion. Note also how different process models — design thinking, agile — frame iteration, since the vocabulary differs even when the underlying cycle is similar.

Implementing Redesign Changes in Prototypes

Introduction

Once usability testing is finished and the findings are sorted, the real work begins: turning what you learned into a revised prototype that can be tested again. This topic walks through the practical decisions that revision involves — how much fidelity a change actually requires, how to translate an observed problem into a specific interface change, how to annotate what you changed and why, how to keep versions organized so you can compare and roll back, and how to sanity-check changes before putting them in front of users again.

Fidelity is a judgment call, not a default. A confusing label or a missing confirmation step may only need a sketch or a wireframe edit, while a problem with flow, timing, or feedback usually has to be felt by a user, which means you need a clickable prototype with real transitions and states. The tooling matters here because it determines how cheaply you can make a change, show it to someone, and change it again.

The videos below focus on the prototyping craft in Figma: building clickable flows, connecting frames with interactions, and using interactive components to handle states such as hover, selected, toggled, or error. While watching, pay less attention to the specific visual styling and more to the mechanics that support iteration — how prototype connections are created and edited, how components let you change one thing and have it propagate everywhere, and where you could attach notes or duplicate a page to preserve an earlier version for comparison.

Documenting and Communicating Redesign Outcomes

Introduction

Usability work does not end when you finish testing or even when you ship a revised interface. The final professional step is documenting what you found, what you changed, and why — in a form that teammates, stakeholders, and future designers can actually use. That means usability testing reports that tie claims to evidence, before-and-after comparisons that make the change visible, and metrics tracked consistently across iterations so improvement (or regression) is measurable rather than anecdotal.

Communicating those outcomes is its own skill. The people who approve your redesign are often not interface specialists, so findings must be framed in terms they care about: task success, time on task, error rates, user frustration, and business consequences. At the same time, you have an ethical obligation not to overstate what a small study shows. Selective quoting, cherry-picked clips, and percentages drawn from five participants can mislead an audience that has no way to check your work.

The three short videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-standing usability research and consulting firm. As you watch, pay attention to three things: how they define usability in measurable components you can report on, how they recommend handling participant quotes so they illustrate rather than distort, and what responsible presentation of study data looks like when the sample is small. Try mapping each recommendation to a section you would write in your own redesign report.
