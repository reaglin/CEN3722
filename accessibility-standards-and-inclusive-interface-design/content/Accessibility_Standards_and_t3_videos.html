Introduction

Not everyone uses a mouse. People with motor impairments such as tremors, limited fine motor control, or paralysis may rely on a keyboard, a switch device, or voice control that emulates keystrokes. Screen reader users also navigate almost entirely by keyboard. For all of these users, an interface that can only be operated by pointing and clicking is simply unusable, which is why WCAG 2.1 requires that all functionality be available from a keyboard.

This topic covers four related ideas. Keyboard operability means every control — buttons, menus, form fields, modals, custom widgets — can be reached and activated with keys like Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, and the arrow keys, and that focus is never trapped. Logical tab order and focus management mean the sequence of focus follows the visual and logical reading order, that focus is visibly indicated at all times, and that it is moved deliberately when dialogs open and close. Skip navigation links let keyboard users jump past long repeated menus straight to the main content. Finally, custom components built from generic elements must reproduce the keyboard behavior that native controls provide for free.

The three videos below are practical, demonstration-based walkthroughs rather than lectures. As you watch, pay attention to the specific keys being pressed, to whether the focus indicator is always visible, and to the moments where the presenter points out a breakdown — focus disappearing, order jumping unexpectedly, or a control that cannot be activated. Try the same steps on a site you use often; you will likely find barriers within a minute or two.

How These Videos Support the Learning Outcomes

Together these videos move from standards to practice. The WCAG references in the ExpandTheRoom walkthrough connect keyboard operability to the formal guidelines named in MO1, while all three model a hands-on inspection method that you can apply when auditing an interface for motor-related barriers, supporting MO2. The specific failure patterns they highlight — missing focus indicators, illogical tab order, unreachable custom controls — give you a concrete punch list for revising your own prototypes under MO3. Finally, seeing a real user journey conducted entirely by keyboard and screen reader makes the cost of exclusion tangible rather than abstract, which is the reflective work asked of you in MO4.
