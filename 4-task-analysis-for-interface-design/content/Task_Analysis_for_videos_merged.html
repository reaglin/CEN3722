Foundations of Task Analysis

Introduction

Task analysis is the practice of breaking down what people actually do to accomplish a goal, step by step, before deciding what an interface should look like. Instead of starting with screens, buttons, or layouts, you start with the user's goal and the sequence of actions, decisions, and judgments that lead to it. Once that structure is clear, the interface can be organized to match it rather than forcing users to reorganize their work to match the software.

This topic sets up the vocabulary you will use for the rest of the module. A goal is the end state the user wants; a task is the work done to reach it; and tasks decompose into subtasks and operations. Some of those steps are observable (clicking, typing, scanning a label, picking up a document), while others are cognitive (recalling a value, comparing options, deciding whether an action moved them closer to the goal). Observable steps can be watched and timed; cognitive steps have to be inferred through questioning, think-aloud protocols, or structured inspection, which is why task analysis uses more than one method.

As you watch the videos below, focus on three things. First, notice how each one separates the user's goal from the mechanics of the path taken to reach it. Second, watch for where the invisible, in-the-head work shows up — the moments where a user has to know, remember, or decide something. Third, think about how each representation (a journey, a flow, a walkthrough question set, an interaction cycle) could be converted into a hierarchical breakdown of the kind you will build later in this module.

Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA)

Introduction

Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA) is a structured way of describing what people actually do when they use a system. You start with a high-level goal, break it into subgoals and operations, and keep decomposing until the steps are concrete enough to be useful for design. The result is usually drawn as a numbered tree or written as an indented outline, with plans that state the order and conditions under which subtasks are carried out.

HTA matters in this course because interface requirements come from tasks, not from guesses. Once you have decomposed a workflow, you can see where steps are repeated, where users must remember information between screens, where errors are likely, and which tasks deserve shortcuts or automation. HTA also gives you a shared notation for discussing a workflow with teammates and stakeholders before any screens are designed.

The videos below approach decomposition and task modeling from different fields: instructional task analysis, hierarchical fault-tree modeling in process improvement, and UML use case diagrams in software analysis. As you watch, focus on the transferable mechanics: how a goal is stated, how far the decomposition is taken, how levels are numbered or labeled, and how the finished diagram is read back as a description of work. Then ask yourself how each representation would translate into an HTA tree with plans for an interface you are designing.

Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA)

Introduction

Hierarchical Task Analysis tells you what users do and in what order. Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA) asks a harder question: what is going on in the user's head while they do it? CTA methods surface the knowledge, cues, judgments, and decisions that experts rely on but rarely state out loud — the things that never appear in a procedure manual yet determine whether a task succeeds or fails.

In this topic you will look at what CTA adds beyond HTA, the core techniques used to elicit expert knowledge (structured and probing interviews, critical decision method, think-aloud protocols, concept mapping), how to locate decision points and reconstruct users' mental models, and how to represent findings in forms a design team can act on — decision requirement tables, concept maps, annotated task flows. The payoff comes in the last subtopic: turning those findings into concrete interface requirements, such as what information must be visible at a decision point, what should be defaulted, and where the interface should prevent a predictable error.

The videos below do not teach CTA procedures directly; they build the two skills CTA depends on. The first two are about questioning technique — how to probe beliefs, reasoning, and assumptions without leading the person you are interviewing, which is exactly the skill a CTA interview requires. The third is about cognitive bias, which matters both because users' mental models are shaped by biases you will have to design around, and because your own biases can distort what you hear in an interview. As you watch, keep translating: when you hear a question type, ask yourself what it would reveal about a user's decision at a step in a workflow you are analyzing.

Comparing and Selecting Analysis Methods

Introduction

By this point in the module you have seen two ways of describing what users do: hierarchical task analysis (HTA), which decomposes observable goals into subgoals, operations, and plans, and cognitive task analysis (CTA), which digs into the knowledge, judgments, and decisions behind expert performance. Neither method is universally better. HTA is efficient, easy to diagram, and excellent for procedural, well-structured work, but it can flatten the reasoning behind a step. CTA surfaces mental models, cues, and decision strategies, but it is interview-intensive, depends on access to experts, and produces findings that are harder to standardize.

Choosing between them (or combining them) is a scoping decision. Before you pick a method you need to settle what question you are answering, who your users are, how much access and time you have, and how deep the analysis needs to go. A narrow scope with a clear boundary (one workflow, one user role, one goal) usually produces more usable design implications than an ambitious analysis that is never finished. The same discipline applies to any research method you will use later in the course.

The videos below place task analysis in the wider landscape of UX research methods, so you can see how method selection is reasoned about in practice: matching a method to an objective, a budget, and a timeline. As you watch, keep translating the general advice into your own decision — which method would you use for your project workflow, what would you have to give up, and how would you know the analysis is deep enough to stop? The third video shows a specific task-based inspection technique that pairs naturally with the task models you are building.

Data Collection for Task Analysis

Introduction

Task analysis is only as good as the data behind it. Before you can diagram a workflow or identify where users struggle, you need an accurate record of what people actually do, in what order, with what information, and what they are thinking while they do it. This topic covers the main ways that raw data is gathered: observing users in their real context, interviewing them and collecting verbal protocols such as think-aloud, mining documentation that already exists (manuals, training materials, logs, help tickets, procedures), and choosing participants who genuinely represent the target user population.

Each method has trade-offs. Observation captures what people do but not always why; interviews capture intent and rationale but are filtered through memory and self-presentation; existing documentation describes how a task is supposed to be done, which often differs from how it is really done. Skilled practitioners combine methods and treat disagreements between them as findings rather than noise. Participant selection matters just as much: data from the wrong users produces a clean, well-structured, and wrong task analysis.

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group and focus on the verbal-protocol and moderation side of data collection. As you watch, pay attention to how a moderator elicits information without putting words in the participant's mouth, what a repeatable session procedure looks like, and when thinking aloud distorts the very behavior you are trying to record. Then consider how the data you collect this way would feed into the hierarchical and cognitive task analyses you build later in the module.

Producing Task Analysis Documentation

Introduction

A task analysis is only useful to a design team if it is written down in a form other people can read, question, and build from. In this topic we look at what a complete task analysis document contains: a statement of scope and users, the data sources behind it, hierarchical task diagrams with numbered plans, narrative task descriptions, and a section that translates findings into design implications and interface requirements. Clear writing matters here as much as correct method — each task description should name the actor, the goal, the trigger, the steps, and the conditions for success, in language a teammate or client can follow without you in the room.

Documentation also has to be validated. Before you hand it to a design team, you check it back against real users and real evidence: did you observe or interview people doing the work, does the hierarchy match what they actually do, and does an independent reviewer reading your document reach the same conclusions? From there the document becomes a bridge to design artefacts — personas, scenarios, screen flows, and requirement lists — and for this course, to your group project deliverable.

The three videos below do not describe a documentation template directly; they cover the evidence-gathering and review practices that make the documentation trustworthy. As you watch, listen for things you can cite in your own document: how qualitative research data is collected and organized, how a session is set up so the data is usable, and how a structured review produces findings that are specific enough to act on. Ask yourself after each video: what would I write down, and in what section of my task analysis document would it go?

Applying Task Analysis to the Group Project

Introduction

This topic takes the methods you learned earlier in the module — hierarchical task analysis (HTA) and cognitive task analysis (CTA) — and puts them to work inside your group design project. The practical questions here are different from the textbook ones: What is the single primary goal your users are trying to accomplish? Who on the team talks to users, and who writes up the notes? How do four people build one coherent task hierarchy instead of four partial ones? And how do interview findings about reasoning, judgment, and error become real content in your project document rather than an appendix nobody reads?

The work breaks into a sequence. First you narrow a broad project idea into a specific, researchable user goal. Then you divide data collection so that observation, interviews, and documentation review are covered without duplicated effort. Next you assemble the HTA as a team, agreeing on decomposition level and plans. Finally you fold CTA findings into the document and review it as a group for consistency, traceability, and clear design implications.

The three videos below come from Nielsen Norman Group, a long-standing UX research and consulting firm. None of them is specifically about HTA, so watch them for the surrounding project skills: how to sharpen a vague research goal into a specific one, how to decide what matters most when you cannot analyze everything, and how to reduce the administrative overhead that eats team time. As you watch, keep asking how each idea would change the way your team plans its next week of project work.
