Introduction

A cognitive walkthrough is a usability inspection method in which evaluators — not real users — step through a task in an interface and ask a fixed set of questions at every action a first-time user would have to take. Instead of asking "is this interface pretty?" or "is this expert-efficient?", the method asks a narrower question: could someone who has never seen this screen before figure out what to do next, and would they know their attempt worked? That focus makes it a learnability test you can run on a paper sketch, a wireframe, or a half-built prototype.

The method rests on a theory of exploratory learning: people often skip manuals and tutorials and instead learn a system by looking at the screen, forming a goal, scanning for something that looks like it matches that goal, acting, and then interpreting the feedback. The walkthrough questions mirror that loop. Understanding this theoretical basis matters in this course because it tells you why the questions are worded the way they are, and it keeps you from drifting into generic opinion-giving when you evaluate a classmate's prototype.

It also helps to see where cognitive walkthrough sits among other inspection methods. Heuristic evaluation, for example, is also expert-driven and cheap, but it sweeps broadly across an interface against a list of principles, while a walkthrough drills narrowly into one task sequence from a novice's point of view. As you watch, note three things: the specific questions asked at each step, the assumption that the user is new to the system, and how the output differs from what a heuristic review produces.
