Defining Project Scope in HCI Design

Introduction

Every interface project begins with a question that is easy to skip and expensive to ignore: what exactly are we building, for whom, and within what limits? Project scope is the agreed boundary around your work. It states the goals the interface must achieve, the constraints you must design within (time, budget, team skills, device capabilities, accessibility and platform requirements), the users you are designing for, and the criteria that will tell you whether you succeeded. Without that boundary, teams drift, features multiply, and the final product satisfies no one in particular.

In this course, scoping is the step that makes the rest of Module 2 possible. You cannot sensibly compare mobile, web, and desktop platforms until you know what your users need to do, where they will do it, and what your project can realistically support. The formal project description you write is both a planning tool and a communication tool: it aligns your team, and later it gives you something concrete to evaluate your design against.

The three videos below approach scoping from different angles. As you watch, pay attention to how each one frames decisions: What questions get asked before design work starts? Who is in the room? How are goals separated from solutions? Where do constraints enter the conversation? Try to collect a short list of questions you could ask about your own project, and note any vocabulary (goals, requirements, deliverables, users, affordances) you can reuse when you write your scope document.

Overview of Target Application Platforms

Introduction

Before you design an interface, you have to decide where it will live. This topic surveys the four primary platforms used in this course — Android, iOS, Windows, and Web — and then widens the view to additional platforms you may encounter in practice, such as macOS, Linux, wearables, smart TVs, automotive displays, and embedded or kiosk systems. Each platform brings its own screen sizes, input methods, interaction conventions, distribution channels, and user expectations, and those differences drive real design decisions.

It also helps to think in categories rather than product names. Mobile platforms assume touch, small screens, interruptions, and on-the-go use. Desktop platforms assume keyboard and mouse, larger screens, and longer focused sessions. Web platforms assume a browser, variable screen sizes, and near-universal reach at the cost of some device integration. Recognizing which category your project falls into is the first step toward justifying a platform choice in your scope document.

The videos below are practical tours of widely used platform ecosystems. As you watch, pay less attention to the specific button clicks and more to the platform-level questions: What device and input method is this software designed for? Does it run in a browser, as an installed desktop application, as a mobile app, or as all three? How does the interface change across those versions, and what does that suggest about the constraints a designer must respect on each platform?

Android Platform: Characteristics and Constraints

Introduction

Android is the most widely deployed mobile platform in the world, and that reach comes with a particular set of design realities. Unlike a single tightly controlled device family, Android spans thousands of hardware models, screen sizes and densities, OS versions, and manufacturer skins, and it reaches users across a very broad range of regions, budgets and network conditions. Designing for Android therefore means designing for variability rather than for one idealized device.

This topic looks at three things: what the Android platform offers (its component model, system UI patterns, and the Material Design language that sets user expectations), the conventions Android users have learned to rely on (navigation structures, standard components, back behavior, theming and typography), and the constraints you must plan around (fragmentation, performance and memory limits, permissions and privacy, accessibility, and store quality expectations). These constraints are not technical trivia — they shape what your interface can reasonably promise.

The videos below come from the official Android Developers channel. As you watch, listen for the distinction between what the platform gives you for free and what you must decide deliberately. Note how often the answer to a design question on Android is "use the standard component unless you have a strong reason not to," and ask yourself what that implies for the scope and effort estimates in your own project.

iOS Platform: Characteristics and Constraints

Introduction

Choosing iOS as a target platform means accepting a particular design philosophy along with a particular set of rules. Apple publishes the Human Interface Guidelines (HIG), maintains a consistent system of controls, typography, materials, and navigation patterns, and reviews every app before it reaches the App Store. For an HCI designer, that combination is both a gift and a constraint: much of the interaction vocabulary is already decided for you, users arrive with strong expectations about how things should behave, and deviating from convention tends to cost you more than it gains.

In this topic we look at three things: what the iOS platform actually is (devices, system design language, and the audience that uses it), the design conventions that make an app feel "native" on iOS, and the constraints that come with Apple's more controlled ecosystem — review policies, hardware and device-size assumptions, accessibility expectations, and limited freedom to invent your own UI patterns. These factors feed directly into whether iOS is the right platform for your team's project.

The videos below come from Apple Developer's WWDC sessions, which is as close to a primary source as you can get for iOS design. While watching, note the recurring vocabulary — hierarchy, consistency, materials, controls, navigation — and ask yourself two questions: what does this guidance require of a designer, and what would it prevent you from doing? Those answers become constraints you can cite in your scoping document.

Windows Platform: Characteristics and Constraints

Introduction

The Windows desktop remains one of the most widely deployed application platforms in business, education, and engineering settings. Unlike a phone, a Windows machine typically assumes a large screen, a mouse or trackpad, a full keyboard, multiple overlapping windows, and long working sessions. Those assumptions shape everything from how much information you can place on a screen at once, to how you expose commands (menus, ribbons, toolbars, right-click context menus, keyboard shortcuts), to how users expect to move data between applications.

In this module you are deciding which platform fits your project. Windows is a strong choice when users need dense data views, precise pointer input, file system access, long-form content creation, or integration with an organization's existing productivity and identity systems. It is a weaker choice when your users are mobile, occasional, or touch-first. The constraints matter as much as the capabilities: varying screen sizes and DPI settings, accessibility and keyboard navigation expectations, installation and update paths, and the strong conventions users already carry from Office and other Windows software.

The videos below approach Windows from three angles: the Microsoft 365 ecosystem that many Windows users live inside, the current developer tooling story on Windows, and a low-code route to building Windows-friendly business apps. While watching, note the interaction patterns you see repeatedly (ribbons, panes, command bars, keyboard shortcuts), and ask yourself what each one assumes about the user's hardware, attention, and environment. Those observations are the raw material for the platform justification and scope document you will write.

Web Platform: Characteristics and Constraints

Introduction

The web is the most broadly reachable application platform available to you. A web app runs inside a browser, which means a single codebase can be opened on a laptop, a tablet, a phone, or a shared kiosk without anyone installing anything. That reach comes with trade-offs: you inherit the browser's capabilities and limits, you depend on a network connection, and you must design for screen sizes and input methods you cannot predict in advance.

In this module you are deciding which platform fits your project. To make that call for the web, you need a working picture of three things: how web applications are typically structured (a client in the browser talking to a server over HTTP APIs), the design conventions users already expect from web pages, and the constraints that shape what a browser-based interface can realistically do. Those three threads map directly onto the subtopics for this topic.

The videos below approach the web platform from three angles: the communication layer that connects a web front end to its back end, the difference between responsive and adaptive approaches to varying screens and capabilities, and practical visual conventions for web layout. As you watch, keep asking what each idea implies for your own project proposal: what would it cost you in effort, what would it give your users, and where would the browser get in your way.

Platform Selection: Criteria and Decision-Making

Introduction

Choosing a platform is one of the first real design decisions you make in an HCI project, and it constrains almost everything that follows: screen size and input method, interaction patterns, offline behavior, accessibility options, distribution, and even how users discover your app. In this topic we treat platform selection as a structured decision rather than a preference. You weigh who your users are and where they will realistically use the product, what capabilities the interface actually requires (camera, sensors, notifications, large-screen multitasking, keyboard-heavy entry), what your team can actually build with the tools and time available, and then you write the decision down with the reasoning behind it.

That last step matters more than students expect. A platform choice that is documented with criteria, alternatives considered, and trade-offs accepted can be defended to a client or instructor, revisited when requirements change, and reused as input to your scope document. An undocumented choice usually turns into an argument later in the term.

The videos below come from software architecture and system design rather than from HCI directly. Watch them for the decision-making method, not for the specific technologies: notice how each one starts from requirements and constraints, lays out options, names the trade-offs, and arrives at a justified choice. Then practice translating that same discipline to your own question — mobile, web, or desktop — where the constraints are users, context of use, and interface capability instead of servers and databases.

Design and Development Tools by Platform

Introduction

Once you have a rough idea of what your project should do and who it is for, the next practical question is: what will you actually build it with? Every platform — Android, iOS, Windows, and the web — comes with its own expected toolchain, from design and prototyping software through to IDEs, UI frameworks, and testing tools. Choosing well early saves rework later; choosing poorly can lock your team into a workflow that fights your design goals or your available skills.

This topic surveys those options in two layers. The first is design and prototyping: tools like Figma that let you sketch, wire up, and test an interface before any code exists, so usability problems surface cheaply. The second is implementation: the native tool sets for each platform (Android Studio and Kotlin, Xcode and Swift, Visual Studio and .NET, plus the browser-based web stack) and the cross-platform frameworks such as Flutter, React Native, .NET MAUI, and Compose Multiplatform that promise one codebase across several targets.

As you watch, keep your own project in mind. Note what each tool assumes about your team's skills, what it makes easy versus awkward, and how much control it gives you over platform-specific interaction conventions. Pay attention to the trade-offs discussed around cross-platform work — code reuse, performance, access to native UI patterns — because those trade-offs are exactly the kind of justification you will need to write into your scope document.
