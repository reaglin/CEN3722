Introduction to HCI Development Reporting

Introduction

An HCI development report is the written record that accompanies an interface project from first requirements to final evaluation. It is not a diary of everything you did; it is a structured, selective account of what you set out to build, what you learned about users, which design decisions you made, and what evidence supports those decisions. In professional practice this document is read by teammates, managers, clients, and sometimes auditors or future maintainers, so it has to stand on its own after you have left the room.

In this course the report matters for two reasons. First, it is how you demonstrate that your project decisions were reasoned rather than accidental — a claim like "we moved the checkout button" is only credible when it is tied to a usability finding or an HCI principle. Second, the report is itself a design artefact: it captures traceability, showing a reader the chain from requirement to design to evaluation to revision. Audience and tone follow from that purpose, since a report written for a mixed technical and non-technical readership must explain jargon, lead with findings, and keep the evidence visible.

The three videos below are short, practice-oriented pieces from Nielsen Norman Group. As you watch, do not look for a report template; look for how experienced practitioners frame design work for an audience. Notice what they choose to include and leave out, how they move from user problem to decision to outcome, and how evaluation methods produce the kind of findings you will later need to cite in writing.

Requirements Documentation

Introduction

Requirements documentation is the written record of what a system must do, how well it must do it, and why those decisions were made. In HCI work this record begins with user research: who the users are, what they are trying to accomplish, and what problems they currently face. From that evidence you derive functional requirements (specific behaviors and features the interface must support) and non-functional requirements (qualities such as responsiveness, accessibility, learnability, and error tolerance). You also capture design constraints — platform limits, standards, budgets, deadlines, accessibility rules — along with the rationale explaining where each constraint came from.

This matters in CEN3722 because your course project report has to do more than describe a finished interface. A reader should be able to trace a line from a research observation, to a stated requirement, to a design goal, to the design decision that satisfies it. When that chain is missing, design choices look arbitrary; when it is present, your report becomes a defensible argument supported by user data.

The three videos below come from the Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established UX research and consulting firm. As you watch, pay attention to three things: what information belongs in a written plan or spec versus what stays informal, how documentation survives in fast-moving project settings, and how precise language reduces ambiguity for the people who have to build and evaluate the design. Try to map each practice you see onto a section of your own project requirements document.

Design Process Documentation

Introduction

Design process documentation is the written and visual record of how an interface came to be: the ideas you generated, the sketches and low-fidelity prototypes you tested, the reasoning behind each choice, the higher-fidelity versions you built, and the way all of this changed across iterations. In practice, a design document is not a scrapbook of pretty screens — it is an argument. It shows a reader what problem you were solving, what alternatives you considered, why you kept one and discarded others, and what evidence supported that call.

This matters in CEN3722 because your course project is judged largely through what you write about it. Graders, teammates, and future maintainers cannot watch you work, so your report must make the invisible parts visible: the discarded concepts, the trade-offs, the usability findings that forced a redesign. Clear documentation of design rationale is also what separates a defensible HCI decision from a personal preference.

The three videos below come from practicing designers and from the Nielsen Norman Group. As you watch, pay attention to the artifacts each one produces at every stage — diagrams, annotated prototypes, specification notes, explanation slides — and ask how you would capture that same artifact in a section of your own report. Also notice how each speaker explains a choice to someone who was not in the room; that framing is exactly what your rationale sections need.

Evaluation Documentation

Introduction

Evaluation documentation is the part of an HCI report that makes your usability work verifiable. It records what you did (the method, the participants or evaluators, the tasks, the setting), what you found (the specific problems, with evidence and severity), and what you changed as a result (the design iterations your findings triggered). Without this record, evaluation results become opinion; with it, they become justification that a reader can trace from a user's struggle to a concrete design decision.

In this course, the evaluation section is where your project report earns its credibility. Your audience — instructors, teammates, and in professional practice, stakeholders and developers — needs to know how confident to be in each finding and what to prioritize first. That requires writing methodology precisely enough to be repeated, reporting findings in a structured and rated way rather than as a loose list of complaints, and stating honestly what your evaluation could not tell you.

The three videos below come from the Nielsen Norman Group and cover complementary pieces of this workflow: running and recording a heuristic evaluation, rating and reporting findings by severity, and reasoning about the limits of small-sample testing. While watching, note the vocabulary each video uses for method steps and severity criteria — borrow that vocabulary for your own report — and keep asking: what would I need to write down during this activity so that someone reading my report later could follow my reasoning?

Final Implementation Documentation

Introduction

Final implementation documentation is the part of your HCI design report that answers a simple but demanding question: what did you actually build, and why does it look and behave the way it does? It includes a clear description of the delivered interface (screens, components, navigation, key interactions), a record of the decisions you made while implementing it, an honest account of where the final product departs from your prototype, and a trace that links each significant design choice back to requirements, user data, or evaluation findings.

This matters in CEN3722 because your project is assessed not only on the artifact but on the reasoning behind it. Reviewers, instructors, teammates, and future maintainers rarely have access to your hallway conversations; the document is the only durable evidence that your choices were deliberate rather than accidental. Deviations from the prototype are normal — time limits, technical constraints, and usability test results all force changes — but undocumented deviations look like mistakes, while documented ones look like engineering judgment.

The video below approaches documentation from the outcomes side rather than the screens side. As you watch, think about how you would phrase your own design decisions in terms of the user outcome each one was meant to produce, and how that framing would make the "tracing design decisions to outcomes" section of your report easier to write and easier for a reader to verify.

Group Collaboration and Contribution Documentation

Introduction

When you submit a group development report, your instructor is reading two things at once: the quality of the interface you designed, and the quality of the process your team used to get there. This topic focuses on the second part — how to document who did what, how decisions were made collectively, and how the project was planned and tracked over the term. Good collaboration documentation is specific and verifiable: it names roles, points to artifacts (meeting notes, task boards, design critiques, version histories), and explains how individual contributions fit into the whole.

There are four things a strong report usually covers. First, team roles and responsibilities — who owned research, wireframing, prototyping, usability testing, and writing, and how overlaps were handled. Second, the collaborative design process — how the team moved from research to sketches to prototypes to evaluation, and where group critique or shared decision-making changed the design. Third, project management activities — scheduling, task assignment, milestones, risks, and how scope was adjusted. Fourth, reflection — an honest account of what worked in the group process and what you would do differently.

The videos below come at this from two angles. Two are project management tutorials that give you concrete, reusable structures for recording responsibility and project scope; the third walks through a UX design process end to end, which is the kind of narrative your report needs to tell. As you watch, pay less attention to the specific tools being shown and more to the structure: what categories of information are captured, what level of detail is useful, and how each artifact could become a labeled section or table in your own group report.

Report Structure, Referencing, and Presentation

Introduction

An HCI development report is more than a collection of findings — it is a document that a reader who was not part of your project should be able to pick up and follow from problem statement to design decisions to evaluation results. That readability comes from structure: a predictable sequence of sections (introduction, methods, findings, discussion, conclusion, references, appendices), each doing a distinct job, with transitions that carry a single argument across them. In this course, your project report is the main artefact that records what you designed, why you designed it that way, and what users told you.

Two supporting skills make that report credible. The first is referencing: when you claim that your layout follows a usability heuristic or that your task-completion metric is a standard measure, you must point to the source, both in the text and in a reference list. The second is presentation — consistent headings, readable typography, properly numbered and captioned figures, screenshots, wireframes, and tables that are referred to in the prose rather than dropped in silently.

The three videos below cover these skills from complementary angles: general report structure, APA formatting of a full document, and the mechanics of in-text citation. While watching, note the function of each section rather than just its name, and pay attention to the small conventions — caption placement, citation punctuation, heading levels — that distinguish a professional report from a rough draft. Also check your assignment brief, since your instructor may specify a referencing style or section list that differs from the examples shown.
