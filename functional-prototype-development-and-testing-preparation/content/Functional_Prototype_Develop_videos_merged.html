From Mockup to Functional Prototype

Introduction

A high-fidelity mockup shows what an interface will look like; a functional prototype shows what it will do. In this topic we examine that shift: what actually makes a prototype "functional," why polished static screens are not enough once real users sit down with your design, and how to decide when your team is ready to move from visual design tools into interactive or coded builds.

The practical pressure point is usability testing. If a participant taps a region that was never wired up, or needs data that your mockup only fakes with a screenshot, the test stops producing useful findings and starts producing apologies. That is why scoping matters: you do not build everything, you build the paths your test tasks require, at a fidelity that lets participants behave naturally and lets you observe where they struggle.

As you watch the videos below, keep three questions in mind. First, which parts of a design are being made real versus merely depicted? Second, what kinds of user behavior can each level of fidelity reveal, and what does it hide? Third, how would you trim a full product vision down to a testable slice that still implements the core features from your own project requirements?

Transition Strategies from Design to Code

Introduction

Moving from a polished mockup to working code is one of the most error-prone steps in interface development. Designs that look complete on a canvas often leave gaps: What happens when a field is empty? What exactly is the spacing between list items? Which screen comes next after a failed login? Transition strategies from design to code are the practices that close those gaps before coding begins, and that keep the build honest once it is underway.

In this module you are building a functional prototype of your own design, so you are both the designer and the developer handing off to yourself — or to a teammate. Four practices matter here: preparing assets and specifications so nothing has to be guessed, building reusable components first rather than screen-by-screen, reviewing the build against the design in short cycles, and catching design drift when implementation choices quietly diverge from the agreed interface.

The videos below demonstrate these practices in Figma, the tool most commonly used for this workflow. As you watch, pay attention to what information the designer deliberately attaches to a file — annotations, flow diagrams, spacing values, exported assets, naming conventions — and ask whether your own project file carries enough of that information for someone to code from it without asking you questions. Also notice how components and styles in the design map onto reusable units in code; that mapping is the backbone of a component-first build.

Development Environment Setup and Use

Introduction

Before you can build a functional prototype, you need a place to build it. A development environment is the combination of tools you install and configure on your own machine — a language runtime, a framework, an editor, a package manager, a version control system, and a way to preview your work as you change it. Setting this up deliberately, rather than improvising, saves hours of confusion later and makes it possible for teammates to run the same project without surprises.

In this course the environment matters because your prototype has to be reachable by real test participants. That means choosing a technology stack you can actually finish in the time available, tracking your work in version control so changes are recoverable and attributable, running a local server with live reload so you can iterate quickly on interface details, and finally deploying a build somewhere participants can open it on their own devices.

The videos below approach this from three angles: a hands-on front-end setup walkthrough, a conceptual map of back-end options, and a focused look at isolating project dependencies. As you watch, pay attention to the setup steps themselves — what gets installed, what commands are run, what a project folder ends up looking like — and to the reasoning behind each tool choice. Ask yourself which parts your team's prototype actually needs and which are unnecessary complexity for a prototype that only has to survive a usability session.

Designing User Proficiency Surveys

Introduction

Before a participant ever touches your prototype, you need to know something about them. A user proficiency survey is a short pre-test instrument that captures a participant's background, their familiarity with relevant technology, and their knowledge of the task domain. Without it, usability data is hard to interpret: a confusing task result might reflect a flawed interface, or it might simply reflect a participant who has never used software of that kind before.

In this topic you will decide which proficiency dimensions actually matter for your project (for example, frequency of use of similar tools, years of domain experience, device preferences, accessibility needs), then write questions that measure those dimensions without leading, biasing, or confusing the respondent. You will also think about structure and administration: question order, response scales, survey length, and when and how the survey is delivered relative to the test session.

The three videos below come from UX practitioners and focus on the craft of asking questions well. As you watch, pay close attention to the specific wording problems they flag — leading questions, double-barreled questions, vague scales, assumptions baked into the prompt — and keep a running list of fixes you can apply directly to your own pre-test survey draft.

Designing Usability Testing Protocols

Introduction

A usability testing protocol is the written plan that makes a user study repeatable. It spells out who you will recruit, what tasks participants will attempt, what the facilitator will say at each stage, what data you will capture (time on task, errors, completion, think-aloud comments, satisfaction ratings), and the practical details of where and how sessions will run. Without this document, two facilitators running the "same" study will produce results you cannot compare.

In this course, the protocol is the bridge between the prototype you are building and the evidence you will collect about it. Your task scenarios must be realistic and goal-oriented rather than instructions that give away the interface; your script must keep you neutral so you do not accidentally coach participants toward success; and your logistics plan must account for consent, recording, timing, and backup options if the prototype breaks. Pilot testing the whole package with one or two people almost always exposes confusing wording and tasks that take far longer than you estimated.

As you watch, pay attention to the exact phrasing facilitators use when introducing a session, the order of steps a moderator follows, and the distinction between testing a product's usability and testing whether users want the product at all. Try to map what you see back to specific sections of the protocol document you will write for your own project.

Integrating Prototype and Testing Preparation

Introduction

Up to this point in the module you have been building a functional prototype and drafting a user testing plan as if they were two separate deliverables. In practice they are one system: every task you ask a participant to perform must be supported by something that actually works in the prototype, with realistic data already loaded and the interface in a known starting state. Integrating prototype and testing preparation means walking through your protocol task by task and confirming that the build can carry it, trimming scope where it cannot, and deciding in advance what the prototype should look like the moment a participant sits down.

This topic covers three practical concerns. Aligning prototype scope with test tasks is about mapping each task script step to a screen, control, or flow that exists; anything unsupported either gets built, faked deliberately, or cut from the protocol. Preparing test data and prototype state covers seeding accounts, sample content, and default settings so sessions are comparable, plus the reset procedure between participants. Coordinating team roles across development and testing means naming who finishes the build, who facilitates, who takes notes, and who owns the pilot run that catches problems before real participants arrive.

The three videos below approach preparation from complementary angles: a research-practice checklist of pre-session work, a designer's end-to-end walkthrough of one usability study, and a set of best practices for running sessions well. While watching, keep your own project in mind and note anything that implies a prototype requirement — a piece of test data, a reset step, a role to assign, or a task you may need to narrow.
