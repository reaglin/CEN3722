Introduction to Figma and the Prototyping Workflow

Introduction

Figma is a browser-based interface design tool that lets you move from a blank canvas to a clickable, shareable prototype without leaving one file. In this topic you will get oriented to the workspace itself: the toolbar, the layers and assets panel on the left, the design and prototype panels on the right, and the canvas where frames live. Knowing where things are is the prerequisite for everything else in this module, because later work on components, auto-layout, and interactions all happens through those same panels.

Beyond the interface, this topic establishes how you will organise and share your work. Figma files contain multiple pages, which is how most teams separate exploration, wireframes, final mockups, and flow diagrams; consistent naming of pages, frames, and layers makes a file readable to teammates and graders alike. Sharing is link-based, with view, comment, and edit permissions, plus a presentation mode for running a prototype — this is how you will submit and demo your project work.

The through-line is the module workflow: wireframe or mockup first, then connect frames with prototype links and transitions, then test and share. As you watch, pay attention to when each presenter switches from the Design tab to the Prototype tab, how they select a hotspot and drag the connector to a destination frame, and which interaction and animation settings they adjust. Try to replicate each step in your own file rather than watching passively — the tool is learned through repetition.

Frames, Grids, and Screen Layout

Introduction

Every screen you design in Figma lives inside a frame. A frame is a container with its own boundaries, background, and layout rules, and it is what Figma treats as a "screen" when you move into prototyping. Learning to create, size, and nest frames well is the first practical step toward a prototype that behaves predictably rather than one that falls apart the moment you resize something.

On top of frames sit two tools that give your layouts structure: layout grids (columns, rows, and uniform grids that keep spacing and alignment consistent across screens) and constraints (rules that tell child objects how to anchor and stretch when their parent frame changes size). Device presets and safe areas connect these ideas to real hardware, so your mobile screen is actually the size of a phone and your important content is not hidden behind a notch or a home indicator. Finally, arranging frames on the canvas in a readable order turns a pile of screens into a flow that a reviewer or teammate can follow.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: where the presenter clicks to create or select a frame versus a group, how constraints are set in the right-hand panel and what changes when the frame is resized, and how grids are used to justify spacing decisions instead of eyeballing them. Try pausing and reproducing each step in your own file — these skills are muscle memory, and they are much faster to learn by doing than by watching.

Building a Component Library

Introduction

A component library is a collection of reusable interface elements — buttons, input fields, cards, navigation bars, icons — that you define once and then reuse throughout a prototype. In Figma, you create a main component and then place instances of it wherever it is needed; editing the main component updates every instance at once. This is the practical mechanism behind the design principle of consistency: instead of manually keeping twelve buttons looking alike, you maintain one definition and let the tool propagate changes.

For this course, components matter because your interface project will likely involve many screens that share the same controls. Building a small library early saves time, reduces visual inconsistencies that confuse users, and makes late-stage changes feasible rather than painful. Variants extend this idea by grouping related states of the same element — default, hover, pressed, disabled — under one component, so you can swap states from a tidy set of properties rather than hunting through a sprawling layer list.

As you watch the three videos below, pay attention to four things: how an existing selection is converted into a main component and what visually distinguishes a main from an instance; how variants are organized and named so the property controls read sensibly; how component properties let you expose text, booleans, and nested instances for swapping; and where components live once created — the Assets panel and, for shared work, a published team library. Try each step in your own file as you watch; components are easier to understand by building than by reading about.

Auto-Layout for Responsive Elements

Introduction

Auto-layout is Figma's system for building frames that arrange their children automatically along a horizontal or vertical axis. Instead of nudging rectangles and text into position by hand, you declare how a frame should behave: how much space sits between items, how much padding surrounds them, how children align, and whether the frame hugs its contents, fills available space, or stays a fixed size. The result is a button that grows when its label gets longer, a card that stretches to fit a longer description, and a list that reflows when you add or remove a row.

This matters in CEN3722 because your prototype should survive contact with real content. Interfaces that only look right with placeholder text tend to break when a user name, product title, or error message is longer than you planned. Auto-layout also pairs naturally with components: a well-built auto-layout component can be reused across screens, swapped, and resized without redrawing anything, which keeps your interface consistent and your file manageable.

As you watch, pay attention to four things: where auto-layout is applied (parent frame versus nested child frames), which sizing mode each element uses and why, how spacing and padding values are set, and how the presenter tests the layout by changing content or dragging the frame's edges. Try to rebuild each example yourself rather than only watching — auto-layout is much easier to understand through your own clicks than through description. Note also that Figma's auto-layout has gained features over time, so menu names and panel positions in older videos may differ slightly from the current interface.

Interactive Prototyping and Connections

Introduction

A static set of screens can show what an interface looks like, but it cannot show how it behaves. Figma's Prototype tab is where you turn frames and components into something a person can actually click through: you select a layer, drag a connection to a destination, and then specify the trigger (what the user does), the action (what the prototype does in response), and the animation that carries the change on screen. Once those connections exist, you can run the file in Presentation mode and share a link so reviewers and usability-test participants can explore the flow on their own.

In this course, prototyping is how your design becomes testable. Evaluation methods you study later — heuristic walkthroughs, think-aloud sessions, task-based testing — all depend on a prototype that responds realistically enough that users forget they are looking at a mockup. Details matter here: a modal that appears over its parent screen, a list that scrolls while the navigation bar stays fixed, a button that visibly changes on hover. These are the affordances and feedback cues users rely on, and in Figma you have to wire them deliberately.

The three videos below come from Figma's own channel and move from building reusable interactive components, to timing-based transitions that run without user input, to overlay behavior for things like modals and expanding thumbnails. As you watch, pay attention to which trigger each presenter chooses and why, how the animation type (instant, dissolve, smart animate, move in) changes the perceived meaning of a transition, and how a small amount of setup in a component can remove dozens of repetitive connections from your file. Try to rebuild at least one example yourself rather than only watching.

High-Fidelity Design and Visual Polish

Introduction

High-fidelity design is the stage where a grey-box wireframe becomes something that looks and feels like a real product. The structural work is already done — boxes, spacing, and flow — and now you decide what the interface actually looks like: colour palettes, type scales, photography and fills, icons, and subtle effects such as shadows, blurs, and overlays that communicate elevation and focus.

This matters in CEN3722 because visual properties are not decoration; they carry meaning. Type size and weight tell users what to read first, colour signals state and status, consistent icons make actions recognisable, and a shadow suggests that something is clickable or floating above the page. Figma supports this through styles (and variables) — reusable definitions for colour, text, and effects — so that a change made once propagates everywhere, which is the practical foundation of a design system.

The three videos below focus on the styles side of high-fidelity work, with the deepest coverage on typography and text hierarchy. While watching, pay attention to how each presenter decides what to turn into a reusable style versus what stays a one-off local setting, how styles are named and organised, and how font size, weight, and spacing are used to build a readable hierarchy. Treat the techniques as transferable: the same create-once-reuse-everywhere logic applies to colour styles, effect styles, and the image fills and icon assets you add to your own project.

Building and Submitting the Group Project Prototype

Introduction

This topic is where everything you have practiced in Figma comes together as a team deliverable. Rather than learning a new tool feature in isolation, you will plan the scope of your group project, agree on a screen inventory, split the build across team members, and then move through a structured cycle of self-review, peer evaluation, and iteration before submitting a shareable prototype link.

The practical challenge in group prototyping is consistency. When four people build screens in the same file, buttons drift in size, colors multiply, and flows stop connecting. Shared libraries, reusable components, and a clear naming and page structure are what keep a multi-person file coherent, and they also make design QA much faster because you can check one component instead of twenty copies of it. Equally important is the collaborative side: mapping flows together before anyone starts pushing pixels, and giving and receiving feedback in a form your teammates can actually act on.

As you watch, look for three things. First, how shared components and libraries let a team work in parallel without the design falling apart. Second, how to safely test and update a component after feedback arrives, so iteration does not break screens other people built. Third, how a collaborative whiteboard can be used up front to agree on scope, user flows, and task assignments, and later to collect peer evaluation notes in one visible place.
