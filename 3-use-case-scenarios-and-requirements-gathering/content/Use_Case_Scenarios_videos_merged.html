Introduction to Use Case Scenarios

Introduction

A use case scenario is a structured description of how a particular user (an actor) interacts with a system to accomplish a specific goal. Instead of listing features in the abstract, a use case walks through the sequence of steps the user takes, what the system does in response, what must be true before the interaction starts, and what counts as success. In human-computer interaction work, this makes use cases a bridge between the people you are designing for and the interface you will eventually build.

In this course, use cases matter because they are where user research turns into something designers and developers can act on. Before you sketch a screen or run a usability test, you need a clear statement of who the user is, what they are trying to do, and under what conditions. Use cases also feed directly into requirements gathering: each step and alternate flow in a scenario tends to reveal a functional requirement, and the conditions around it often reveal non-functional ones such as speed, accessibility, or error tolerance.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to four things: the vocabulary (actor, goal, precondition, postcondition, main flow, alternate and exception flows), how a written use case description differs from a use case diagram, how use cases connect to requirements documentation, and how use cases compare with user stories in agile practice. Try to notice that these are complementary tools rather than competitors — a short user story captures intent, while a full use case captures detail.

Identifying Stakeholders and Users

Introduction

Every interface is built for someone, and almost every interface is also paid for, approved, maintained, or regulated by someone else. Before you can write a believable use case scenario, you have to know who those people are. This topic introduces stakeholder analysis: the practice of systematically listing everyone who has an interest in a system, classifying them, and deciding whose input shapes which design decisions.

A key distinction in this module is between users and stakeholders. Users are the people who directly operate the interface to accomplish a task; stakeholders are the broader set of parties affected by or invested in the system, including managers, clients, support staff, compliance officers, and sometimes people who never touch the software at all. Users are a subset of stakeholders, but their goals are not identical to stakeholder needs — a manager may need audit trails and cost control while the frontline user needs speed and fewer clicks. You will also look at how user roles and permissions subdivide the user population, since an administrator and a guest may use the same system in very different ways.

As you watch the videos below, keep a running list of two things: the techniques each speaker uses to surface stakeholders who might otherwise be forgotten, and the kinds of questions used to pull needs and goals out of those people. Notice that the videos come from different traditions — UX research, project management, and software engineering — and that each tradition frames stakeholders slightly differently. That contrast is useful: it shows you that stakeholder identification is a transferable skill with vocabulary that shifts depending on who is doing it.

Requirements Gathering Techniques

Introduction

Before you can design an interface, you need to know what people actually need it to do. Requirements gathering (also called requirements elicitation) is the systematic process of drawing out those needs from stakeholders and users: what goals they are trying to accomplish, under what conditions, with what constraints, and what would count as success. The techniques in this topic — interviews, contextual inquiry, workshops and focus groups, surveys and questionnaires, and analysis of documents and existing systems — are the standard toolkit, and each one answers a different kind of question.

In this course the payoff is immediate: the raw material you collect here becomes the actors, goals, preconditions, and step-by-step flows of the use case scenarios you write later in the module. Vague or assumed requirements produce vague use cases and, eventually, interfaces that solve the wrong problem. Gathering well also helps you sort what you learn into functional requirements (what the system must do) and non-functional ones (speed, accessibility, learnability, reliability).

As you watch, pay attention to three things. First, matching method to question — when is observing a user at work better than asking them, and when is a survey the right scale of tool? Second, the mechanics of good questioning — how practitioners avoid leading, biasing, or interrupting participants. Third, what the output looks like: try to imagine, for each technique, the kind of notes or data you would walk away with and how you would turn those notes into a use case step.

Task Analysis

Introduction

Task analysis is the systematic study of what users actually do to accomplish a goal. Instead of guessing at features, you observe or interview people, then break their work into a structured set of steps, decisions, and sub-goals. The result is a task model: a description of the user's goal, the sequence of actions needed to reach it, the information required at each step, and the conditions under which the sequence changes.

In this module, task analysis is the bridge between raw requirements-gathering data and the use case scenarios you will write. Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA) decomposes a goal into numbered sub-tasks and plans, giving you a tree you can read almost directly into main flows and alternate flows. Cognitive task analysis goes after the invisible part — what the user must know, remember, notice, or decide — which is where most interface difficulty actually lives. Once you have a model, you validate it by walking users back through it and correcting the steps you got wrong.

The three videos below approach the topic from different directions: one on task analysis as a decomposition and teaching technique, one on cognitive walkthrough as a way to evaluate a task step-by-step from the user's mental perspective, and one on hierarchical breakdown structures from project management. While watching, focus on the mechanics: how a goal is split, how deep the decomposition goes, how steps are numbered and ordered, and how you would know whether the resulting model is correct. Note also where each technique stops — none of these videos is a complete HCI task-analysis method on its own, so read them as complementary pieces.

Writing Use Case Scenarios

Introduction

A use case scenario is a written description of how a user (the actor) interacts with a system to accomplish a goal. Rather than describing screens or code, it records the sequence of steps that move a user from an intention to a result, along with the conditions that must hold before the interaction starts and the state the system is left in afterward. In this module you will learn the structure of a use case document: identifying the actor and goal, specifying preconditions and postconditions, writing the main success scenario, and then documenting alternative flows (other valid ways to reach the goal) and exception flows (what happens when something goes wrong).

This matters in an HCI course because use cases are the bridge between the users you study and the interface you design. They force you to commit to a concrete interaction context, expose decisions and error states that are easy to overlook, and give you a vocabulary that designers, developers, and stakeholders can all check against. A well-written use case is also a source of testable requirements, which is why it connects directly to the requirements work in the rest of this module.

As you watch the videos below, pay attention to three things: the template or section headings used, how each step is phrased (short, numbered, one actor action or one system response per step, no ambiguous words like "etc." or "appropriately"), and where the author branches out of the main flow into an alternative or exception. Try pausing and predicting the next step before it is shown, then compare your phrasing to the example on screen.

Enumerating Usage Paths and Edge Cases

Introduction

When we design an interface, it is tempting to describe only the "happy path" — the sequence of steps a cooperative user takes when everything works. Real systems are messier. Users lose network connection halfway through a form, enter names with apostrophes or characters your field rejects, switch languages, use a screen reader, hold the phone in one hand on a moving bus, or abandon a task and return three days later. Enumerating usage paths means systematically walking through a feature and listing every branch it can take, including the rare, boundary, and failure branches that we call edge cases.

This topic builds directly on the use case scenarios you have been writing. A use case set is only as good as its coverage: if an important path is never written down, it will never be designed for, never be tested, and will surface as a defect or a frustrated user. You will practice techniques for generating paths (varying inputs, states, sequences, interruptions, and contexts), for considering user diversity (ability, expertise, language, device, environment), for recording how often each path occurs and how much it matters, and for reviewing a set to judge whether it is reasonably complete.

The videos below approach this from three angles. As you watch, look for: how designers discover paths they did not anticipate (by talking to and observing real users), how a use case diagram makes alternate and exceptional flows visible as explicit structure, and how the needs you uncover get sorted into functional versus non-functional requirements — because many edge cases (performance under poor connectivity, accessibility, error recovery) land on the non-functional side of that line.

Documenting and Communicating Requirements

Introduction

A use case that lives only in the author's head, or in a file nobody can find, has no value to a project. Once you have written scenarios and gathered requirements, the work shifts to documentation and communication: where use cases are stored, how they are named and versioned, how each one connects back to the requirements it satisfies, and how you keep all of it current as the product changes. This topic treats use cases and requirements as shared project artifacts rather than one-time deliverables.

Three practices hold this together. Organization means a repository with consistent structure, identifiers, and status so team members can locate and reuse what exists. Traceability means every requirement can be followed forward to the use cases, design elements, and test cases that realize it, and backward to the stakeholder need that justified it. Communication means presenting use cases in a form that clients, developers, and testers can each act on, and keeping stakeholders involved when things change.

As you watch, look for three things: how a formal specification document is structured and what sections it contains; how traceability links are created, visualized, and checked for gaps; and what concrete behaviors make stakeholder collaboration work rather than fail. Note also how design and test activities draw directly on these documents, since that downstream use is the main reason to invest in clean documentation.
