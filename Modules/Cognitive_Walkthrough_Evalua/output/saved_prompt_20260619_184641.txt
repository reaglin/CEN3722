TOPIC 1: Foundations of the Cognitive Walkthrough Method
  • What Is a Cognitive Walkthrough?
  •   It is an inspection method, meaning it does not require real end users to participate.
  •   The method focuses specifically on learnability — how easily a first-time user can accomplish tasks.
  •   Evaluators walk through each interface action and assess whether a novice user would understand what to do next.
  •   It was developed by Polson, Lewis, Rieman, and Wharton in the early 1990s as a cognitive science-based evaluation technique.
  • Core Theoretical Basis: Exploratory Learning
  •   Users tend to rely on recognition and contextual clues rather than memorized instructions.
  •   Good interface design should support users in making correct guesses about what to do next.
  •   Feedback and visible affordances are central to helping exploratory learners succeed.
  •   The method evaluates whether the design supports this natural learning behavior at every step.
  • Learnability as the Primary Focus
  •   Learnability measures how quickly a novice user can reach a basic level of performance.
  •   The method is most valuable for interfaces expected to be used by first-time or infrequent users.
  •   Recurring users who will eventually memorize workflows are a lower priority for this method.
  •   Evaluators must maintain the perspective of a user who has never seen the interface before.
  • Cognitive Walkthrough vs. Other Inspection Methods
  •   Heuristic Evaluation is faster but less targeted at specific task flows.
  •   Cognitive Walkthrough produces findings tied to concrete user actions and decision points.
  •   Both methods can be applied to prototypes before any user testing occurs.
  •   Combining both methods increases the breadth and depth of usability issues discovered.
  Notes: Introduces the theoretical basis and goals of Cognitive Walkthrough as a usability inspection technique focused on learnability and user cognition.

TOPIC 2: Preparing for a Cognitive Walkthrough
  • Defining the User Profile
  •   The user profile should specify domain knowledge, technical familiarity, and motivation.
  •   Evaluators must consistently adopt this persona throughout the entire walkthrough.
  •   Assumptions about prior knowledge directly affect whether a failure is flagged or overlooked.
  •   User profiles can be drawn from personas or user research conducted earlier in the design process.
  • Identifying Task Sequences
  •   Tasks must be written from the user's perspective, not in terms of interface controls.
  •   Each task should have a clearly defined starting point within the interface.
  •   Tasks should represent high-priority or high-frequency user goals.
  •   Breaking complex tasks into numbered sub-steps is essential for granular evaluation.
  • Gathering Required Materials
  •   A documentation template ensures consistent recording across all tasks and evaluators.
  •   All interface screens relevant to the selected tasks must be available before starting.
  •   Evaluators should not modify the prototype during the walkthrough.
  •   Having the user profile written down prevents drift toward expert assumptions mid-evaluation.
  • Assembling the Evaluation Team
  •   Solo evaluations are practical and commonly used in academic and professional settings.
  •   Team evaluations benefit from discussion at each step to surface differing interpretations.
  •   All evaluators must apply the same user profile and task definitions for results to be comparable.
  •   Evaluators should have basic familiarity with usability principles but not be designers of the product being evaluated.
  Notes: Covers the essential preparation steps required before beginning the walkthrough, including defining users, selecting tasks, and gathering materials.

TOPIC 3: The Step-by-Step Cognitive Walkthrough Procedure
  • Overview of the Four Evaluative Questions
  •   The questions are applied sequentially to every individual action step in the task.
  •   A 'failure' answer to any question signals a potential usability problem at that step.
  •   All four questions must be considered even if earlier questions suggest a failure.
  •   The questions shift evaluator focus from interface aesthetics to user cognition and decision-making.
  • Question 1 — Will Users Try to Achieve the Right Goal?
  •   Evaluators must ask whether the design communicates the necessary sub-goal clearly.
  •   Users may have the right ultimate goal but form an incorrect intermediate goal.
  •   Vocabulary mismatches between user language and interface labels are a common source of failure here.
  •   If this question fails, the interface likely needs clearer framing or labeling of the action.
  • Question 2 — Will Users Notice the Correct Action Is Available?
  •   Controls that are visually prominent and contextually placed are more likely to be noticed.
  •   Users scan interfaces rather than reading every element, so salience matters greatly.
  •   Failure here often suggests a need for improved visual hierarchy or placement.
  •   An action that exists but cannot be found is functionally equivalent to an action that does not exist.
  • Question 3 — Will Users Recognize the Action as Correct?
  •   Labels and icons must clearly communicate what will happen when the action is taken.
  •   Users rely on prior experience and conventions to interpret unfamiliar controls.
  •   Failure here suggests the need for clearer labeling, tooltips, or contextual help.
  •   This question distinguishes between noticing an element and understanding its function.
  • Question 4 — Will Users Understand the Feedback They Receive?
  •   Feedback should confirm success, signal errors, or indicate that a process is underway.
  •   Ambiguous or absent feedback leaves users uncertain and likely to repeat or abandon actions.
  •   Error messages should explain what went wrong and suggest corrective action.
  •   Positive feedback reinforces correct behavior and builds user confidence in the interface.
  Notes: Walks through the core four-question framework applied at each action step, which forms the operational heart of the Cognitive Walkthrough method.

TOPIC 4: Identifying and Documenting Failure Points
  • What Constitutes a Failure Point?
  •   A failure is a step where the design does not adequately support the user's cognitive process.
  •   Failures can range in severity from minor confusion to complete task abandonment.
  •   The same interface element can produce failures on multiple questions simultaneously.
  •   Evaluators should record failures even when they seem minor, as they may compound across steps.
  • Categorizing Failures by Type
  •   Categorizing failures by question type reveals whether problems are structural or surface-level.
  •   Multiple failures of the same type suggest a systemic design problem rather than isolated errors.
  •   Severity ratings (e.g., low, medium, high) help prioritize which failures to address first.
  •   Categorization makes findings more actionable for the design team.
  • Writing Effective Failure Descriptions
  •   Include the task number, step number, and the interface element involved in every entry.
  •   Describe what the user would likely think or do incorrectly, not just what the interface does wrong.
  •   Link each failure to a specific evaluative question for traceability.
  •   Suggest a potential redesign direction where possible, though this is optional.
  • Using a Documentation Template
  •   Templates reduce the cognitive load of documentation during the evaluation itself.
  •   Standardized formats allow findings from multiple evaluators to be merged and compared.
  •   Every evaluated step should be recorded, not just those where failures occur.
  •   Completed templates serve as a formal deliverable and audit trail of the evaluation process.
  • Distinguishing Severity Levels
  •   Critical failures prevent task completion and must be addressed before release.
  •   Moderate failures create frustration and reduce user confidence even if the task is eventually completed.
  •   Minor failures are worth noting but may be addressed in later design iterations.
  •   Severity should reflect the impact on the defined novice user, not the evaluator's own experience.
  Notes: Explains how to recognize, categorize, and record usability failures discovered during the walkthrough for use in design improvement.

TOPIC 5: Applying the Method to Peer Prototypes
  • Understanding the Peer Prototype Evaluation Context
  •   Findings should be framed as observations about the interface, not judgments about the designer.
  •   Objectivity is maintained by consistently applying the user profile and four questions, not personal preference.
  •   Evaluators should complete the walkthrough independently before discussing findings with the prototype's creator.
  •   Peer evaluations provide valuable real-world practice that mirrors professional UX evaluation contexts.
  • Selecting Tasks for Each Prototype
  •   Each task should reflect a realistic goal a user of that prototype would actually have.
  •   Tasks should be decomposed into the smallest observable actions before the walkthrough begins.
  •   If multiple tasks are evaluated, prioritize those with the highest frequency or consequence of failure.
  •   Document your task definition as part of your submitted evaluation report.
  • Conducting the Walkthrough on Each Prototype
  •   Begin each walkthrough from the defined starting state of the task, not from the prototype's home screen unless specified.
  •   Apply all four questions to every step, even when no failure is apparent.
  •   Note any assumptions you make about what a novice user would think or know.
  •   Record both failures and successes to provide a complete picture of the interface's strengths and weaknesses.
  • Comparing Findings Across Three Prototypes
  •   Identify whether any failure categories appear consistently across multiple prototypes.
  •   Note design decisions that successfully supported user cognition for use as positive examples.
  •   Cross-prototype comparison strengthens your ability to recognize systemic usability patterns.
  •   Reflections on cross-prototype findings should be included in your final evaluation summary.
  • Preparing and Submitting the Evaluation Report
  •   Each prototype should have its own clearly labeled section within the report.
  •   Executive summaries should highlight the most critical failures and their potential impact.
  •   All claims about failures must be traceable to specific steps and evaluative questions.
  •   Submit completed documentation templates as appendices to support your written findings.
  Notes: Guides students through the independent application of the Cognitive Walkthrough to three real peer-created prototypes, including practical considerations and ethical conduct.

TOPIC 6: Interpreting Results and Communicating Findings
  • Synthesizing Findings into Patterns
  •   Group failures by the evaluative question they violate to surface recurring design weaknesses.
  •   Patterns across multiple steps within one task suggest a structural interface problem.
  •   Patterns across multiple tasks suggest a broader design philosophy issue.
  •   Synthesized findings are more useful to designers than long lists of isolated issues.
  • Writing Actionable Recommendations
  •   Recommendations should be directly tied to specific failure types and their root causes.
  •   Avoid recommending complete redesigns when targeted changes would resolve the failure.
  •   Prioritize recommendations by the severity of the failures they address.
  •   Where possible, reference established design conventions or guidelines that support your recommendation.
  • Presenting Findings to Designers
  •   Lead with the most critical findings to ensure they receive attention regardless of time constraints.
  •   Use user-centered language that focuses on the novice user's experience, not the evaluator's opinion.
  •   Provide evidence for each finding by referencing the specific task step and evaluative question.
  •   Acknowledge successful design decisions alongside failures to provide balanced, credible feedback.
  • Limitations of the Cognitive Walkthrough Method
  •   Findings reflect the evaluator's simulation of a novice user, not actual novice user behavior.
  •   The method does not capture issues related to long-term learnability or expert-level efficiency.
  •   Evaluator expertise and assumptions can introduce bias into failure judgments.
  •   Cognitive Walkthrough findings should ideally be validated through subsequent user testing.
  Notes: Covers how to synthesize walkthrough findings into actionable recommendations and communicate them clearly to designers and stakeholders.
