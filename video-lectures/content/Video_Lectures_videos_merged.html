Human Cognition and Perception

Introduction

Every interface you design is used by a person with a limited and predictable set of cognitive abilities. We perceive only part of what is in front of us, we attend to even less, and we can hold only a handful of items in mind at once before they decay or get overwritten. Theories of human information processing — perception, attention, working memory, long-term memory, and the time course of feedback — give us a vocabulary for explaining why some interfaces feel effortless and others feel exhausting.

In this course, these ideas are the justification behind many design rules you will otherwise have to memorize. Recognition over recall, chunking, consistent placement, visible system status, and short feedback latencies are not style preferences; they are responses to documented limits in human cognition. Understanding the underlying mechanism lets you reason about novel design situations instead of guessing.

The videos below come from the Nielsen Norman Group, a long-established usability research and consulting firm. As you watch, note two things: first, the specific claim about human capability being made (how much, for how long, under what conditions), and second, the concrete interface implication drawn from it. Also notice how the third video shifts from describing cognition to using a cognitive model as an evaluation tool — that bridge from theory to method is exactly what we are after.

Knowledge, Mental Models, and Conceptual Frameworks

Introduction

Every time you sit down in front of an unfamiliar app, you are not starting from zero. You bring prior knowledge: general world knowledge, knowledge of similar software, knowledge of conventions (a trash can deletes, an underlined blue word links), and procedural habits built from years of clicking, tapping, and swiping. From this knowledge you assemble a mental model — an internal, often incomplete and sometimes wrong, account of what the system contains and how it behaves. That model is what you use to predict what a button will do before you press it.

Designers cannot hand users a mental model directly. What they can do is express a conceptual model through the interface: the objects, actions, relationships, and metaphors the design makes visible. Interface metaphors (desktops, folders, carts, feeds) act as cognitive bridges, letting users borrow structure from something familiar. When the conceptual model communicated by the interface matches the system's real behavior, users form accurate mental models quickly; when it does not, they form confident but wrong models and make systematic, repeatable errors.

As you watch these videos, look for three things. First, what cues in the interface are doing the model-building work — spatial arrangement, direct manipulation of visible objects, labels, animation, explanations. Second, how mental models change over time as users accumulate experience with a system. Third, how early impressions can distort a model in ways that persist, which is a direct design concern rather than just a psychological curiosity.

Human Error and Learning

Introduction

People make mistakes, and interactive systems are where many of those mistakes become visible. This topic looks at human error systematically: the difference between slips (doing the wrong thing while intending the right thing) and mistakes (intending the wrong thing in the first place), the conditions that make errors more likely, and the design choices that either invite or prevent them. A recurring theme is that error is rarely just "user fault" — poorly labelled controls, ambiguous feedback, and interfaces that conflict with a user's mental model all produce predictable, systematic failures.

The second half of the topic turns to learning and recovery. Good interfaces are forgiving: they constrain dangerous actions, make the current state visible, offer undo, and explain problems in language the user can act on. They also support a learning curve, letting novices succeed through guidance and discoverability while giving experts faster paths. These goals sometimes pull against each other, which is why designers need principles rather than one-size-fits-all rules.

As you watch the three videos below, keep a running list of two things: (1) the specific error type or cause each example illustrates, and (2) the concrete design move used to prevent, contain, or recover from it. Pay attention to how error handling differs between a form on a large screen and an alert on a small mobile screen — the same principle often requires a different implementation.

Understanding Users and Task Analysis

Introduction

Before you can design an interface, you have to know who will use it, what they are trying to accomplish, and how they think the system works. This topic covers the front end of user-centered design: identifying your actual users (as opposed to the imagined "average person"), researching their needs through interviews, observation, surveys, and contextual methods, and modeling their goals and mental models so that your design matches their expectations rather than the developer's internal logic.

The second half of the topic turns research into structure. Task analysis is the practice of breaking down what users do into describable units: Hierarchical Task Analysis (HTA) decomposes a high-level goal into subtasks and plans that govern their order, while Cognitive Task Analysis focuses on the knowledge, decisions, and attention demands hidden behind observable actions. Both feed directly into design decisions — what to put on a screen, what to automate, what to group together, and where errors are likely.

The videos below focus on the research methods side of this topic. As you watch, pay attention to three things: what kind of data each method produces (what people say versus what people do), what context the data is gathered in, and how a researcher avoids leading or biasing the participant. Try to connect each method back to how you would use its output to build an HTA or a mental-model description for a system you are designing.

Interaction Design and Prototyping

Introduction

Interaction design is the practice of shaping how people and interactive systems communicate with each other: what the system shows, what the user can do, what happens next, and how the user knows it happened. It sits between understanding users (goals, tasks, mental models) and building software, and it is deliberately iterative — you sketch a concept, put it in front of someone, learn what breaks, and revise.

In this course the topic matters for two reasons. First, the core principles (visibility, feedback, constraints, consistency, affordances and signifiers) give you a shared vocabulary for critiquing desktop and mobile interfaces instead of calling them simply "good" or "bad." Second, prototyping is how those principles get tested cheaply: paper sketches, wireframes, clickable mockups and higher-fidelity mockups each trade effort for realism, and each is useful at a different stage. A prototype is also a communication artifact — it lets you show a teammate, client or test participant an idea that words alone would not pin down.

As you watch, keep a running list of three things: concrete examples of designs that succeed or fail and the principle that explains why; the stages of the design process and where prototypes and user feedback enter the loop; and the difference between what an interface affords and what it merely appears to afford. You will use all three when you build your own low-to-mid fidelity prototype.

Usability Evaluation and User Testing

Introduction

Usability evaluation is the part of the design process where you stop guessing and start checking. Instead of assuming an interface works, you apply structured methods — expert inspections, heuristic reviews, and tests with real users — to find out where people hesitate, make errors, or give up. The goal is not to prove a design is good, but to surface problems early enough and cheaply enough that you can fix them.

In this course, this topic ties together everything that comes before it. You have studied memory limits, mental models, and task structure; evaluation is how you verify that your design actually respects those constraints. You will also see two broad families of methods: expert-driven inspection (such as heuristic evaluation), which is fast and cheap but filtered through a reviewer's judgment, and user testing, which is slower but reveals behavior you could not have predicted. Knowing when to use each is a core professional skill.

As you watch, pay attention to three things: how usability is defined and broken into measurable components, how evaluators move from scattered observations to a consolidated, documented list of issues, and how qualitative findings get paired with quantitative metrics. Also notice that evaluation is described as something you repeat — each round feeds a design revision, which then gets tested again.

Human Factors, Ergonomics, and Automation

Introduction

Human factors (also called ergonomics) is the study of how people actually perform within systems — physically, perceptually, and cognitively — and how equipment, displays, software, and work processes should be shaped around those human capabilities and limits. In this topic we look at both halves of the field: the physical dimension (posture, reach, repetitive strain, workstation layout) and the cognitive dimension (attention, memory load, situational awareness, decision making). Both matter to interface design, because an interface is never used in isolation; it sits inside a task, a workstation, and a work system.

The topic also covers automation: what happens when part of a task is handed off to a machine or algorithm. Automation rarely removes work — it changes it, often shifting the human into a monitoring role, which can degrade situational awareness, create new error modes, and leave people out of the loop when something goes wrong. Display design principles (making relevant information visible, grouping it sensibly, matching it to the operator's mental model) are the main lever designers have for keeping people informed and in control.

While watching the two videos below, keep two questions in mind. First, what is the limiting factor in the scenario being described — the body, the senses, attention, or memory? Second, what design or work-system change would relieve that limit, and what new problem might that change introduce? That trade-off thinking is the core habit of human factors work.

Collaborative and Advanced Interface Technologies

Introduction

Most of this course treats interaction as a conversation between one person and one system. This topic widens the frame. Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) studies how groups of people coordinate, communicate, and share artifacts through technology, and groupware frameworks are the tools built to support that work: shared documents, version control, notebooks, messaging, awareness cues, and handoff mechanisms. Alongside CSCW, this topic covers advanced interface paradigms — immersive and spatial displays, mixed reality, mobile and embedded interaction — plus the question of how much a system should automate and how much the human should oversee.

These ideas matter here because the design principles you have been learning do not disappear when the context gets more complex; they get harder to apply. Shared workspaces multiply the number of mental models in play, immersive displays change what information can be presented and how attention is divided, and automation shifts the user's role from doing to monitoring. Advanced technology does not excuse bad task analysis, confusing feedback, or untested prototypes — it raises the cost of getting them wrong.

The three videos below are short conference presentations from ACM SIGCHI, the professional community that produces much of the research in this field. As you watch, listen for how each team defines the collaborative setting, what methods they used to study it, and what design implications they draw. Pay attention to the vocabulary too — terms like teamwork versus taskwork, awareness, workflow, and research agenda are standard in this literature and will help you read further on your own.
